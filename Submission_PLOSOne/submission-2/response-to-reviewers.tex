\noindent Dear Dr.\ D'Acquisto and Reviewers,

\medskip

\noindent Thank you for the careful and constructive reviews. We are grateful for the
time invested, and we think the manuscript is substantially better for the revision.

We should say at the outset that the revision goes further than point-by-point repair. Working
through the reviewers' comments on positioning (R1.1, R2.10), on controls and baselines (R2.8)
and on the reach of our claims (R2.11) led us to a clearer statement of what the framework
offers that existing methods do not. What it offers is a posterior distribution over every gene
field and, from it, over every ligand-receptor interaction field. The revised manuscript makes the
use of that posterior its central result: it asks whether differences that are visible on maps of
the interaction fields are supported by the data, for averages over regions, along transects and
over whole fields, and it sets each answer beside the readings commonly made without a posterior.
To do this we refitted every gene with the published model to obtain joint posterior draws,
which the original analysis had not stored. The regions, transects and examples were fixed before
any posterior comparison was computed. On the section studied, many differences that look clear
on the maps are not supported once the uncertainty, and in particular its spatial correlation, is
accounted for. The analyses that the original submission centered on, the tissue domains and the
controls that the reviewers asked for, are retained in a shorter section, with their full text
in a supporting appendix.

Reviewer 2 asked whether the framework identifies biologically meaningful spatial structure
beyond smoothing, increased feature dimensionality, or clustering. We have addressed that
question directly with three controls (Section R2.8): a coordinate shuffle with full model
refitting, which collapses the domains; a concordance test against the independent anatomical
annotation published with the source dataset; and the ligand-receptor pair-shuffling control
the reviewer suggested, which found no effect of the pairing on domain coherence and which we
report in full, together with the limit it places on our conclusions. The reframed analysis
also answers the reviewer's request for a baseline: every comparison is now set against the
plug-in reading, a test that treats locations as independent, and the posterior with its
spatial correlation removed.

We have also moderated the generalizability and biological-interpretation language
throughout, clarified the statistical model and its implementation, and added a
supplementary table mapping every mathematical object in the manuscript to the code that
implements it.

Reviewer comments are reproduced verbatim in italics, followed by our response. Because the
changes are extensive, a word-level comparison is hard to read; we suggest reading the clean
revised manuscript together with the revision summary that follows this letter, which lists
what moved, what is new and what was removed. The required marked-up manuscript is also
provided. One caveat about that file: the markup covers the prose but not the interiors of
displayed equations, so the revised model specification in the Materials and methods does not
appear visually flagged even though it changed. We describe that change in words under R2.1
below.

\section{Reviewer 1}

\subsection{R1.1. Comparative analysis with existing spatial modeling techniques} \status{DONE}

\begin{rev}
Recommend the authors to perform a comparative analysis with other existing techniques
for spatial modelling techniques as verifications.
\end{rev}

\noindent We have added comparison and validation along four axes. The first is new to this
revision and is now the central result; the other three are described in full in our response to
Reviewer 2's comment R2.8.

First, every comparison the revised manuscript makes between regions, along transects or over
whole fields is set beside the ways the same question is commonly answered without our
posterior: reading the point-estimate map, a Wilcoxon test that treats each location as an
independent observation, and our own posterior with the correlation between locations removed.
Across 19,201 comparisons between pairs of regions (1,477 ligand-receptor pairs, 13 fixed region
comparisons each), the Wilcoxon test finds a difference in 90\%, the posterior with its spatial
correlation removed in 73\%, and the joint posterior in 46\% (Table 2). Of the comparisons that
look different on the maps by a factor of at least 1.25, 31\% are not supported by the posterior.
These counts come from one tissue section and share regions and genes, so they are not rates, but
they show concretely where the framework changes a conclusion that a simpler reading would draw.

Second, within our own framework we compare candidate convolution operators
quantitatively, and this comparison includes the product operator used by CytoSignal.
On this dataset, and in a single clustering run, the common-minimum operator we adopt gave
more clusters and more spatially coherent domains than the product operator. This comparison is
now S15 Fig.

Third, we compare the emergent domains against an external reference that we did not
produce: the anatomical annotations published with the source dataset by Chen et al.
(2022). This is a comparison to an independently derived, expression-based segmentation
of the same tissue section.

Fourth, we added a spatial permutation test with full model refitting, which is the
standard validation device in this literature.

We want to be straightforward about what we have not done. We have not run a full
method-versus-method benchmark against SpatialDE, BOOST-GP, SVCA, SpatialDM, or
CytoSignal. Those methods answer different questions from ours: they test individual
features or interactions for spatial significance, whereas our contribution is a
posterior over continuous fields and its use to judge whether apparent differences are
supported. There is no shared target quantity on which a head-to-head accuracy comparison would
be meaningful without first defining a benchmark task, which we regard as its own piece of work.
We have instead positioned our approach against each of these methods, and against the closest
precedents for the new analysis, explicitly (R2.10), and we state this limitation in the
Discussion rather than leaving it implicit.

\subsection{R2.1. Precision of the statistical model specification} \status{DONE}

\begin{rev}
First, the statistical model is not specified with sufficient precision. The manuscript
introduces a hierarchical statistical framework, but several key parameters are not
clearly defined, including $\theta_i$, $\theta_N$, $\Sigma(\theta_i)$, $\Sigma(\theta_N)$,
the covariance function, prior structure, and the relationship between the abstract
mathematical model and the computational implementation. Since this model is central to
the manuscript, all parameters should be introduced clearly at first use.
\end{rev}

\noindent We accept this criticism. The model section was written at a level of
abstraction that made the framework readable but left the actual fitted model
underdetermined. We have rewritten it to define every symbol at first use and to state
the concrete choices used in the reported analyses.

Specifically, the revised Materials and methods now state that the spatial random effect
for feature $i$ is a zero-mean Gaussian process with a Mat\'ern covariance function,
represented through the stochastic partial differential equation approach, so that
$\theta_i = (\rho_i, \sigma_i)$ collects the range and marginal standard deviation for
feature $i$, and $\Sigma(\theta_i)$ denotes the resulting Mat\'ern covariance matrix
evaluated at the mesh nodes. The smoothness parameter is fixed rather than estimated
($\alpha = 2$, giving Mat\'ern smoothness $\nu = 1$ in two dimensions). We place
penalized-complexity priors on $\theta_i$: the range prior satisfies
$P(\rho_i < 500) = 0.95$ and the marginal standard deviation prior satisfies
$P(\sigma_i > 0.1) = 0.05$, with the range in the same physical units as the chip
coordinates. A sum-to-zero constraint is applied to the field for identifiability against
the intercept. The symbols $\theta_N$ and $\Sigma(\theta_N)$ played a role only in the
latent total-count model, which the final analyses did not use; see R2.3, where we have
clarified their status rather than leaving them ambiguous.

We have also made two changes to the model display itself. The process stage is now written
additively, as $\log\lambda_i(\mathbf{s}) = \alpha_i + u_i(\mathbf{s})$ with $u_i$ a zero-mean Gaussian
process, rather than as a Gaussian process with mean $\alpha_i$. The two are equivalent, but
the additive form separates the intercept from the field, which is how the model is actually
implemented, and it gives the field its own symbol so that it can be named in the mapping
table. We have also corrected a small imprecision: the original text wrote

\subsection{R2.4. Prediction metrics and model comparison} \status{DONE}

\begin{rev}

\end{rev}

\noindent We have added explicit definitions for each metric, stated the orientation of
each, expanded the Table 1 caption to define every abbreviation and the parenthetical
percentages, and stated which criteria we prioritized and why.

In summary: mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) are point-forecast
accuracy measures; the Dawid-Sebastiani score (DS) and logarithmic score (LS) are proper
scoring rules that assess the full predictive distribution rather than a point summary. All
four are negatively oriented, so smaller values indicate better performance. We prioritized
the proper scoring rules, because the manuscript's argument concerns the quality of the
predictive distribution and its uncertainty rather than point accuracy alone.

We have also rewritten the Table 1 caption completely, and in doing so we found and
corrected an error in how the table had been read.

The parenthetical percentages are the mean across features of the ratio of that model's
score to the Poisson score \emph{for the same feature}, so 100\% denotes a model
indistinguishable from Poisson. They are ratios, not differences, and they are not the
ratio of the two averages printed in the table, which is why 0.29 against 0.21 appears as
111\% rather than 138\%. The old caption called them an ``average percentage difference,''
which is a misnomer for a ratio; the new caption defines them correctly and defines every
abbreviation.

The error concerns the Dawid-Sebastiani column, reported in the table as MDS, the mean
Dawid-Sebastiani score. Because MDS is negative, a ratio below
100\% corresponds to a score \emph{nearer zero}, and DS is negatively oriented, so a ratio
below 100\% indicates worse performance rather than better. Reading the 85\% and 82\%
entries as ZIP and ZAP outperforming Poisson, which is the natural reading and one we had
made ourselves while preparing this response, inverts the result. Comparing the raw values
directly, Poisson at $-2.15$ is better than ZIP at $-1.78$ and ZAP at $-1.77$. Poisson is
therefore the best model on all four scores, not three of four, and no criterion disagrees.
The new caption warns the reader about this explicitly and directs them to the raw MDS
values.

On which criteria we prioritized: we prioritized the two proper scoring rules over the
point-forecast measures, because the manuscript's argument concerns the quality of the
predictive distribution rather than point accuracy. As it happens the choice does not
matter here, since Poisson wins on all four.

We have added one piece of new evidence, because the averages alone do not show whether the
result is driven by a few extreme features. Comparing the three likelihoods feature by
feature, Poisson gives the lowest score for 814 of the 912 features on MAE, 880 on RMSE, 734
on MDS, and 829 on MLG, and is best on all four simultaneously for 645 of the 912. These
counts are computed from the same per-feature score files that produce Table 1.

Finally, the caption's claim that averages were taken over ``all 918 modeled
ligand/receptor features'' was wrong in three ways, and is now corrected. The fitted set is
912, not 918. The averages exclude feature-by-model combinations with extreme values
indicating nonconvergence, via the filter \texttt{MAE < 100 \& abs(MDS) < 100}. And that
filter is applied to the absolute scores for one table and to the \emph{ratios} for the

\subsection{R2.8. Controls and baselines} \status{DONE}

\begin{rev}

\end{rev}

\noindent We judged this to be the most important comment in the review, and we have addressed it in
two ways.

The first concerns the reviewer's request for a baseline against which the framework can be
evaluated. In the revised manuscript every comparison of interaction fields, between regions,
along transects and over whole fields, is set beside three baselines: the plug-in map read
directly, a Wilcoxon test that treats each location as an independent observation, and our own
posterior with the correlation between locations removed (Methods; Results, Table 2). This shows
where the framework changes a conclusion, and it separates the effect of ignoring spatial
correlation from the effect of ignoring uncertainty altogether: across 19,201 region comparisons
the three find differences in 90\%, 73\% and 46\% of cases for the Wilcoxon test, the
decorrelated posterior and the joint posterior respectively.

The second concerns the tissue domains, and is the controls from the reviewer's menu: a spatial
permutation test with full model refitting, a comparison with previously annotated tissue regions,
and ligand-receptor pair shuffling. They are summarized in the Results section on the domains and
given in full in the new S16 Appendix; the code and summary outputs are in the public repository.

Throughout, spatial coherence of the resulting domain map is quantified with the
entropy-based local indicator of spatial association (ELSA) computed on the categorical domain
labels, at neighborhood radii of 50 and 100 units. ELSA is negatively oriented: lower
values mean more spatially coherent domains. The domain-generating pipeline is held fixed
across observed and control runs, so any difference is attributable to the manipulation
alone.

\subsubsection*{Control A: coordinate shuffle with full model refitting}

This is the direct test of whether the spatial smoother manufactures coherent structure
from data with no spatial signal. We permuted the assignment of observed counts to spatial
locations, destroying spatial structure while preserving the count distribution exactly,
and then refit every feature's spatial model from scratch on the permuted data before
running the identical convolution, clustering, and scoring pipeline. This required
refitting all 912 features under each of 49 permutations, which we carried out on a
high-throughput computing cluster. Because the clustering then had to be repeated for every
permutation, both the observed and the permuted data were clustered with a faster
implementation of the same steps rather than with Seurat; the pipeline is identical between
the two arms of the control, which is what the comparison requires, and the manuscript now
states this.

The result is unambiguous. The real data yielded 20 domains with ELSA at radius 50 of
0.075. The 49 coordinate-shuffled replicates yielded 4 to 12 domains with ELSA at radius 50
averaging 0.470, ranging from 0.412 to 0.583. Not one of the 49 shuffles came within a
factor of five of the observed coherence; the null mean is 6.3 times the observed value.
The permutation $p$-value is 0.020, which is the smallest value attainable with 49
permutations, so the test is limited by the number of replicates rather than by the
separation between the observed value and the null.

We regard this as the substantive answer to the reviewer's concern. Fitting a spatial
Gaussian process to spatially meaningless data does not produce coherent tissue domains; it
produces a small number of incoherent fragments. The domains we report therefore reflect
spatial structure present in the data, not structure imposed by the smoother.

\subsubsection*{Control B: concordance with independent anatomical annotation}

As a positive control we compared the emergent domains to the anatomical annotations
published with the source dataset by Chen et al.\ (2022) for the same tissue section. These
annotations were produced by a different group, by a different method, and without reference
to our labels, which our pipeline in turn never sees, so they are an independent reference in
the sense that matters for a positive control.

For transparency we also checked and now state how those labels were derived: by spatially
constrained clustering, combining a spatial nearest-neighbor graph with an expression
nearest-neighbor graph, followed by manual labeling of each cluster from its marker genes
against a reference embryo atlas. The comparison is thus between two partitions of the same
tissue section, which is unavoidable, since a concordance check is only meaningful against
annotations of the same specimen. The two partitions are nonetheless built from very
different features, theirs from binned expression across all genes and ours from the
inferred interaction fields of 1477 ligand-receptor pairs, and the manual labeling step brings in
anatomical knowledge from outside the dataset. We note in the manuscript that the two
controls answer different questions: the coordinate shuffle asks whether the structure is
real, and the concordance comparison asks whether the structure that survives is anatomically
meaningful.

All 26{,}000 modeled spatial bins matched an annotated bin. Agreement was substantially
above chance: adjusted Rand index 0.401 and normalized mutual information 0.604, against a
permutation null whose mean adjusted Rand index was 0.00003 and whose maximum over 499
permutations was 0.001, giving $p = 0.002$. The mean size-weighted purity of the emergent
domains with respect to anatomical labels was 77.6\%. Individual domains recover recognized
structures cleanly: spinal cord at 97\% purity, liver at 96\%, one heart domain at 99\%,
meninges at 92\%, with lung, muscle, thymus, and cartilage primordium also recovered.

Again we state the result carefully. An adjusted Rand index of 0.401 is substantial but far
from perfect agreement, and the disagreement is structured rather than random: our
signaling domains subdivide several organs, most visibly the heart, into multiple
territories. We interpret this as the expected consequence of measuring a different
modality. Anatomical annotation partitions tissue by cell-type composition, whereas our
domains partition it by ligand-receptor signaling context, and the two need not coincide.
The appropriate conclusion is that the emergent domains recover major anatomical structures
while resolving additional substructure, not that they reproduce the anatomical
segmentation.

\subsubsection*{Control C: ligand-receptor pair shuffling}

We also ran the pair-shuffling control the reviewer suggests. Receptors were reassigned to
ligands at random, and any reassigned pair that coincided with a real FANTOM5 pairing, or
paired a gene with itself, was then repaired by targeted swaps, so that no shuffled set
retained a real pair. The set of receptors is unchanged, so every shuffled set is built from
exactly the same fitted fields as the true pairing, and only the pairing differs. Each
shuffled set then passed through the same convolution, clustering and scoring as the true
pairing.

Our first attempt at this control could not separate signal from noise: it used the faster
clustering implementation described below in a configuration that did not reproduce exactly
between runs, and the difference between the true pairing and the shuffled null was smaller
than the run-to-run spread. We have since rerun it, in that implementation with a single
thread and a fixed seed, which makes it exactly reproducible, and separately in Seurat, the
implementation behind the original Figures 5 and 6 (now S15 Fig and Fig 9).

The true pairing did not produce more spatially coherent domains than shuffled pairings. In
Seurat, with 19 shuffled sets, ELSA at radius 50 was 0.124 for the true pairing against a
shuffled mean of 0.093 (standard deviation 0.018), and 17 of the 19 shuffles were more
coherent than the true pairing. In the faster implementation, with 99 shuffled sets, the
true pairing sat inside the shuffled distribution, with 24 of the 99 shuffles more coherent.
The direction of the small difference is not consistent between implementations, and we read
the result as no detectable effect of the pairing on domain coherence.

That is not the same as the pairing carrying no information. True pairings produced 1449
numerically distinct interaction fields. This count needs no clustering, so we computed it
for 9,999 shuffled pairings: they produced 1424 on average (standard deviation 5.9), and only
one reached 1449 or more, a permutation $p$ of 0.0002. True partners are also more alike in spatial pattern
than random partners matched for how consistently one partner is the smaller of the two
(mean Pearson correlation 0.111 against 0.068 across the 1477 pairs; paired $t$-test
$p = 4 \times 10^{-21}$), although even random partners are positively correlated, so the
pairing-specific part is modest. Our working interpretation, which we have not tested
directly, is that the common-minimum operator on raw counts passes little of this
information on to the clustering: for 42.6\% of pairs the same partner is the smaller one at
more than 95\% of locations, so the interaction field is largely that partner's field.

We have reported this control in the Results section on the domains and in S16 Appendix, and
added a paragraph to the Discussion stating the limit it places on our conclusions: the domains reflect the collection
of fitted ligand and receptor fields, not the specific pairings. We think this is the most
useful thing the control tells a reader, and we would rather state it than leave the
reviewer to infer it.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility of the clustering, and which implementation was used where}

Two implementations of the clustering step appear in this work, and we have made explicit in
the manuscript which was used where and how reproducible each is. The original Figures 5 and 6 (now S15 Fig and Fig 9) were clustered with Seurat. Control A, and one arm of Control C,
were clustered with a faster implementation of the same two steps (an RcppHNSW
nearest-neighbor graph followed by Louvain clustering in igraph), because Control A needed
the clustering repeated for every permuted dataset. The two give ELSA values on different
scales, so each control compares observed and null within one implementation, and the
manuscript now says which was used where.

The faster implementation is exactly reproducible with a single thread and a fixed seed, and
not otherwise: across eight replicate runs on identical input with four threads and no seed,
ELSA at radius 50 ranged from 0.053 to 0.067. For Seurat we have not established exact
reproducibility. As an indirect check, an independently written script re-ran the published
feature-depth sweep at six depths and at full depth: mean ELSA at radius 50 was within 0.012
of the published value at every depth, and 0.1236 against the published 0.1237 at full
depth, with 19 domains against the published 20. The manuscript now treats each Seurat
domain map as a single realization with an approximate domain count.

\subsubsection*{What the controls establish together}

Control A shows that the spatial structure is real rather than an artifact of smoothing.
Control B shows that the resulting structure corresponds to recognized anatomy, while also
resolving substructure within it. Control C shows that this structure does not depend on the
specific ligand-receptor pairings, which narrows what it says about signaling. Together we believe these address the reviewer's question
of whether the method finds meaningful structure beyond smoothing, dimensionality, and
clustering: the coordinate shuffle rules out the smoothing and dimensionality explanations,
since the shuffled data pass through an identical pipeline of identical dimensionality, and
the anatomical concordance shows the surviving structure is biologically interpretable.

We have not added simulated datasets with known ground truth, the remaining item on the
reviewer's menu. Our reasoning is that a simulation would have to be generated under a
spatial model, and any simulation we could write would be close enough to our own fitted
model that recovering the truth would demonstrate self-consistency rather than validity.
The coordinate-shuffle control tests the same concern using the real data and without that
circularity.

\subsection{R2.10. Scientific positioning relative to existing work} \status{DONE}

\begin{rev}
The scientific positioning of the manuscript should also be strengthened. The authors
should more clearly discuss related work in Gaussian Process-based spatial transcriptomics,
Bayesian spatial count modeling, spatial variance component analysis, and spatial
ligand-receptor inference. Relevant methods such as SpatialDE, BOOST-GP, Spatial Variance
Component Analysis, and SpatialDM should be discussed to clarify which aspects of the
manuscript are novel and which represent combinations, extensions, or reinterpretations of
existing approaches.
\end{rev}

\noindent We have added a positioning subsection that discusses each of these method
families and states plainly which parts of our contribution are new and which are
combinations or reinterpretations of established ideas.

In outline: SpatialDE (Svensson et al., \emph{Nature Methods}, 2018) introduced
Gaussian-process testing for spatially variable genes, decomposing each gene's expression
variance into a kernel-parameterized spatial component and a noise component and testing the
former by a likelihood ratio test. We share its use of a GP prior over space but not its
goal, since we estimate and use the field itself rather than testing features for spatial
variability. BOOST-GP (Li et al., \emph{Bioinformatics}, 2021) is the closest methodological
relative: it places a gene-specific zero-mean Gaussian process on the spatial random effect
inside a zero-inflated negative binomial count model, with Bayesian variable selection over
whether that spatial component is present, and its stated goal is likewise identifying
spatially variable genes. Our contribution relative to it is not the count-GP combination,
which we explicitly do not claim as novel, but the downstream use of the posterior fields as
continuous objects that are convolved and then analyzed jointly. Spatial Variance Component
Analysis (Arnol et al., \emph{Cell Reports}, 2019) decomposes expression variance into
intrinsic, environmental, cell-cell interaction, and noise components and tests whether the
interaction component is nonzero, which is a variance-partitioning aim rather than a
field-estimation one, and it was developed for imaging-based data. SpatialDM (Li et al.,
\emph{Nature Communications}, 2023) tests ligand-receptor pairs for spatial co-expression
using a bivariate extension of Moran's $I$, globally and per spot, with significance from a
permutation null or an analytically derived one; the analytical null assumes that each gene is
spatially randomly distributed. It targets significance of individual interactions where we
target a continuous inferred field per interaction with quantified uncertainty.

We should correct one thing we had wrong in our own preparation, since it affects how we
cite BOOST-GP. Its published title is ``Bayesian modeling of spatial molecular profiling
data via Gaussian process'' and it appeared in \emph{Bioinformatics}; ``BOOST-GP'' is the
software name and does not appear in the journal title. We verified the characterization of
each of these methods against the papers themselves rather than from summary knowledge, as
the reviewer's comment warrants.

We have also added a discussion of CytoSignal (Liu et al., \emph{Nature Genetics}, 2026),
concurrent work that appeared after our submission and that analyzes the same Stereo-seq
mouse embryo dataset. It infers spatially resolved ligand-receptor signaling at cellular
resolution using a score with a spatial-permutation null, and validates against experimental
proximity-ligation ground truth. We do not claim novelty for spatially resolved
ligand-receptor mapping in light of that work, and we have removed language that implied it.

Reading these methods closely also clarified where our contribution lies, and it led to the
reframing described at the start of this letter. None of the four methods the reviewer names,
nor CytoSignal, uses a posterior over continuous fields to judge whether a difference visible on
a map is supported by the data. We therefore read the closest precedents for that question in
full and now discuss them as well: Splotch (\"Aij\"o et al., 2019), a Bayesian count model that
compares anatomical regions one pair at a time through region coefficients; the excursion sets of
Bolin and Lindgren (2015, 2018), applied with the same INLA and SPDE machinery to functional brain
imaging by Mejia et al.\ (2020); SHADE (Eliason et al., 2026), which builds simultaneous credible
bands from joint posterior draws for interaction curves between cell types; SpaceBF (Seal and
Neelon, 2026), which places pointwise credible intervals on ligand-receptor co-expression; and
simulation studies reporting that tests treating locations as independent overstate evidence
under spatial correlation (Vasconcelos et al., 2026; Seal and Neelon, 2026).

Stating our contribution precisely, and more narrowly than the original submission did: we
contribute a generative Bayesian geostatistical treatment of raw spatialomics counts that yields a
posterior for every gene field and, through joint posterior draws, for every ligand-receptor
interaction field; the use of those posteriors, with joint inference, to ask whether differences
visible on interaction-field maps are supported, for regional averages, along transects and over
whole fields, with the regions and examples fixed in advance; and the finding that clustering the
full collection of inferred interaction fields produces tissue domains that correspond to anatomy
(though, as the pair-shuffling control shows, not domains that depend on the specific
ligand-receptor pairings). None of the ingredients is new on its own; the combination, applied to
ligand-receptor interaction fields, is what we offer, and we now say so.

We have also corrected an overstatement of our own that this comparison brought into focus.
Earlier drafts described the uncertainty as propagated through to the interaction fields. In the
original analysis it was not: the convolution and every stage downstream of it operated on
posterior medians, so the interaction fields were point estimates. The revised analysis does
propagate it, for the comparisons. We refitted every gene with the published model to obtain joint
posterior draws, checked that the refits reproduce the published fits, and formed the posterior of
each interaction field draw by draw. The revised manuscript also reports how the plug-in fields
differ from their posteriors (Fig 5): relative to the posterior median of the interaction, the
plug-in field is never lower, and is higher by more than 25\% at 22\% of pair-bin combinations,
most where neither partner is clearly the limiting one, while the spatial pattern changes little
(median correlation over bins 0.988). Carrying the posterior through the clustering itself remains
undone, and the Discussion says so.

All of this now appears in a new Discussion subsection, ``Relationship to existing
methods,'' and all of these works are cited. That subsection also states plainly that we have
not run a head-to-head benchmark against any of them, and why: there is no shared target
quantity on which an accuracy comparison would be meaningful without first defining a
benchmark task and a ground truth, which we regard as a separate piece of work. We would
rather say that than leave the omission implicit.

\subsection{R2.11. Generalizability claims} \status{DONE}

\begin{rev}
Some claims of generalizability appear broader than the evidence provided. The study is
demonstrated using a selected region from a public Stereo-seq mouse embryogenesis dataset.
This is useful as a proof of concept, but spatial omics platforms differ in resolution,
capture efficiency, sparsity, noise structure, segmentation assumptions, field of view, and
biological scale. The authors should moderate broad claims about applicability to most
spatial omics datasets or provide additional analyses and practical guidance for different
platforms.
\end{rev}

\noindent We accept this and have moderated the claims throughout, rather than attempting to
justify the original ones with additional platforms.

We should, however, separate two things that the original text ran together. One is whether
the framework is in principle tied to Stereo-seq, and we do not think it is: the model needs
only counts at known locations with a measure of local sampling effort, and the SPDE
representation of the Gaussian process is built on a triangulated mesh rather than a lattice,
so irregular or hexagonal observation geometries need no special handling. We have separately
fitted fields of this kind to 10x Visium mouse brain sections, a hexagonal spot array at much
coarser resolution, for 2,035 ligand-receptor mechanisms across two sagittal sections, with
posterior predictive occurrence tracking observed occurrence closely across all 3,865 fits
(correlation 0.99, median absolute difference 0.015). The other thing is whether the
\emph{pipeline} has been shown to transfer, and it has not. That earlier work used a two-part
semicontinuous observation model on normalized data rather than the raw-count model developed
here, and it stopped at per-mechanism fields without going through the convolution and
clustering stages. We have added a short passage to the Discussion making exactly this
distinction, and stating that comprehensive evaluation across tissues and platforms remains
future work. We would rather record the limited evidence we have with its limits attached
than either overclaim from it or leave the reader with the impression that the method is
inherently platform-specific.

The revised manuscript states the scope of the demonstration explicitly wherever a general
claim previously appeared: one field of view, from one section, of one embryo, on one
platform, at one binning resolution. Claims of the form ``applicable to spatial omics data''
have been narrowed to describe what we demonstrate, with the extension to other platforms
stated as an expectation and its conditions given rather than asserted.

We have added a short paragraph on what would need to hold for the approach to transfer,
which we think is more useful to a reader than a broad claim: the observations must be
counts with an interpretable exposure or offset, the spatial support must be dense enough
that a Gaussian process is identifiable at the length scales of interest, and at
single-cell-resolution platforms the binning step we rely on would need to be replaced by an
explicit treatment of cell geometry. We also note that platforms with substantially sparser
capture may favor a zero-inflated likelihood, which our Table 1 comparison found
unnecessary at this sparsity but which the framework accommodates.

The specific changes are as follows. The Abstract previously asserted that ``the general
methods piloted here can readily be applied to spatialomics data from diverse platforms with
no need to alter data collection techniques''; it has been rewritten to state what was
actually done and on what, and it now ends by naming the scope of the demonstration and
pointing to the transfer conditions instead of asserting general applicability. The opening
sentence of the model section previously claimed the model ``can be applied to most, if not
all, existing spatialomics datasets''; it now states the property a dataset must have for
the model to apply, and cross-references the Discussion. The Discussion claim that ``our
methods are generally-applicable to any spatial transcriptomic data'' has been replaced by
the paragraph of transfer conditions described above.

One further sentence needed correcting rather than softening. The limitations paragraph
said that ``our general approach has thus far proved easy to adapt on all data types
tested,'' which is not supportable in a manuscript that fits only Stereo-seq data. It now
says that we have fitted the model only to Stereo-seq data here, so our expectation that
adaptation is straightforward is an expectation rather than a demonstrated result.

\subsection{R2.12. Caution in biological interpretation} \status{DONE}

\begin{rev}
Finally, the biological interpretation should remain cautious. I appreciate that the
authors acknowledge that transcript abundance is only used as a proxy for protein abundance.
However, because downstream analyses involve inferred ligand-receptor interactions and
morphogenic signaling fields, the manuscript should avoid language that implies direct
evidence of protein-level interactions, ligand-receptor binding, signaling activity, or
information transmission. Terms such as ``proxy,'' ``inferred field,'' ``model-derived
estimate,'' or ``potential interaction'' would be more appropriate.
\end{rev}

\noindent We agree, and we have revised the language throughout.

We have replaced formulations that implied observed protein-level events with formulations
describing model-derived estimates from transcript measurements. Descriptions of
ligand-receptor quantities now consistently use ``inferred interaction field'' or
``potential interaction'' rather than wording that implies binding was observed; references
to signaling activity are now phrased as transcriptional evidence consistent with potential
signaling; and language implying information transmission between regions has been removed
or explicitly marked as interpretation.

We have also added an explicit statement of the inferential chain and its assumptions in the
Discussion, so a reader can see exactly how far the claims are from the measurement:
transcript counts are a proxy for transcript abundance, transcript abundance is a proxy for
protein abundance, co-localization of ligand and receptor proxies is a proxy for the
opportunity for interaction, and none of these steps is directly observed here.
We now also state the scope of the method plainly, in the Abstract and the Discussion: the
interaction fields estimate the potential for signaling under the co-expression assumption
standard in cell-cell communication analysis, and we propose them as a proxy for signaling
rather than a measurement of it. The Discussion also notes that co-presence is assessed
within a bin of 50 by 50 capture spots rather than at a shared cell surface.

The inferential chain now appears explicitly in the Discussion, in the terms the reviewer
suggests: transcript counts are a proxy for local transcript abundance; transcript abundance
is a proxy for protein abundance, a step we do not attempt to correct; co-localization of a
ligand proxy and a receptor proxy is a proxy for the \emph{opportunity} for interaction; and
none of the intermediate quantities, and no binding event, is directly observed in this
work.

We settled on ``inferred interaction field'' as the single term for the convolved output and
use it consistently. Specific replacements include: a claim that the model outputs
``directly estimate local ligand-receptor binding density'' now says they estimate a proxy
for it; ``the maximum possible number of local binding events'' is now an upper bound
implied by the estimated levels under a stated assumption; ``individual ligand-receptor
binding density field'' in the discussion of the domain figure (now Fig 9) is now ``individual inferred
ligand-receptor interaction fields''; and the invitation to readers to ``observe the true
scale and structure of extracellular communication in mammalian tissues'' now invites them
to explore the scale and structure of the inferred fields. We also removed several
intensifiers that editorialized rather than described, including ``statistically-powerful,''
``experimentally pioneered,'' and two uses of ``significant'' in a non-statistical sense.

\noindent In preparing this revision we audited the feature and interaction counts reported
in the original manuscript against the analysis outputs, and found errors that we have
corrected throughout. We report them here because they change numbers and statements that
appear in the text, in Table 1, and in the original Figures 5 and 6 (now S15 Fig and Fig 9).

First, the attrition from the curated feature list was not described. The list contains 918
ligand or receptor genes. Three (\emph{a}, \emph{Calcb}, \emph{Cckar}) were excluded before
fitting, and three more (\emph{Fgf1}, \emph{Fzd7}, \emph{Trhr}) failed to converge, leaving
912 features with fitted spatial fields. The original text stated that the spatial fields of
all 918 features were modeled. The revised text gives the correct figure and explains the
attrition.

Second, the number of interaction fields was overstated by four. The manuscript reports 1481
unique ligand-receptor interaction fields, and describes them as following from the 918-gene
list. Neither part of that is right. The 1481-row object used for the high-dimensional
clustering contains four exactly duplicated rows, which we traced to files that had been
duplicated in the input files and then read in alongside their originals. The four affected interactions are PDGFC-PDGFRA, PDGFC-PDGFRB, PDGFD-PDGFRA, and
PDGFD-PDGFRB, and in each case the duplicate is numerically identical to its counterpart. The
correct number of ligand-receptor interactions computable from the 912 fitted features is
therefore 1477, which we confirmed by re-deriving it from the FANTOM5 pairings and checking
it against the 1481 rows of the clustered object.

These 1477 interactions are not 1477 numerically distinct fields. Because each field is the
pointwise minimum of its ligand and receptor fields, interactions that share a partner which is
the smaller of the two at every location all reduce to that partner's field. For example,
seven interactions of the ligand GDF9 with different receptors yield one and the same field.
Counting this way, the 1477 interactions give 1449 numerically distinct fields. This is a
property of the minimum operator rather than an error, and we now state it in the manuscript
alongside the count of interactions. Separately, 1481 was never the right figure
for the 918-gene list either: 1493 FANTOM5 pairs have both partners among the 918 curated
genes. We have corrected the count wherever it appears, including the upper limit of the
feature-depth sweep in the domain figure (now Fig 9).

We do not expect this to affect the results materially, but we have not measured it directly.
The duplication gives four of the 1477 interactions twice their intended weight in a clustering
over more than a thousand scaled dimensions. As an indirect check, a re-run of the full-depth
clustering without the duplicated columns, by an independently written script, gave a mean
ELSA at radius 50 of 0.1236 against the published 0.1237, and 19 domains against 20. Because
we have not established that the Seurat clustering is exactly reproducible (see R2.8), that
comparison bounds the combined effect of the duplication and of run-to-run variation rather
than isolating the duplication. The manuscript now discloses the duplication in the Materials
and methods.

Both corrections have been applied to every occurrence in the manuscript text, in the
Table 1 caption, and in the domain figure caption, and to the panel labels inside the domain
figure image, which previously read ``1481 Features''.

Re-deriving the feature-depth experiment (now Fig 9) from its saved outputs also showed that
three statements about it were wrong, and we have corrected them.

The caption said that domains become more numerous as features are added. They become fewer:
33 at 10 features, 24 at 150, and between 16 and 21 from 350 onward. The Results said that the
full feature set gave the greatest spatial coherence of the depths swept. Five deeper depths
(1200 to 1450 features) scored slightly better, although differences of that size are close to
what a re-run of the clustering can produce. The Results also said that clustering without
principal-component analysis gave the greatest complexity and coherence of the configurations
swept. We found no matched comparison with and without principal-component analysis in our
analysis record, so we have removed that claim and now say only that the clustering was run
without it.

Because the published experiment admitted features in order of variance, it confounded the
number of features with which features they were. We have added a control that admits random
subsets of the same sizes (19 per depth). Mean ELSA at radius 50 then falls steadily with
depth, from 0.372 at 10 features to 0.120 at 1000, with most of the change by a few hundred
features. The rise and fall of coherence in the published curve does not appear with random
subsets, which suggests it reflects the order of admission rather than the number of
features. Both results are now in the Results.

Finally, the operator-comparison caption (now S15 Fig) and text said that the common minimum gave the most clusters at
the lowest spatial entropy of the operators tested. It gave the most clusters, but the
geometric mean had slightly lower spatial entropy (ELSA 0.047 against 0.050 at radius 100), a
difference within run-to-run variation. The text now says so, and no longer ranks those two
operators on coherence.

One further finding is disclosed for completeness. To obtain the joint posterior draws used in the
revised analysis we refitted all 790 genes needed by the ligand-receptor pairs with the published
specification, using current versions of the fitting software, since the versions used for the
published fits were not recorded. For 778 genes the refits reproduce the published per-location
posterior medians and interval widths within Monte Carlo error. For 12 sparsely detected genes they
do not, and we could not establish why. Those genes enter 17 of the 1,477 pairs; we flag these pairs
rather than exclude them, and every count in the revised manuscript is also reported without them,
changing none by more than about 1\% (S13 Table).
