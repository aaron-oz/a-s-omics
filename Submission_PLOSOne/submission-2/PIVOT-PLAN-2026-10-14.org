* The new message, in one paragraph

Ligand-receptor signaling fields estimated from spatial transcriptomics are routinely read by
eye: "the signaling field looks different in these two areas". Our model gives every gene field
a full posterior, and therefore every signaling field too. With it we can ask whether apparent
differences are supported by the data, and we find places where fields that look different are
not credibly different, and places where fields that look alike are. We show this for areal
averages over regions, for one-dimensional slices through the tissue, and for whole fields,
using joint (simultaneous) inference as the primary answer. Signaling fields (common-minimum
convolutions) are the primary target, at Sam's request; one gene-field example shows the same
machinery on a single gene.

Scope stays modest: one tissue section, one platform. These are demonstrations of what the
framework allows, not biological findings.

* Decisions taken (AOZ, 2026-10-14)

   clustering and pair-shuffle material shrinks to a shorter section plus Supporting
   Information.
2. Regions: a few fixed simple boxes, and a few regions from the published anatomical
   annotation (Chen et al. 2022, MOSTA). Never our own clustering domains, because those were
   derived from the same data being compared (double dipping).
3. Scale: compare rates per unit of total counts (the model's =lambda=), not expected counts,
   so differences in sequencing depth between regions cannot masquerade as biology.
4. Both same-field comparisons (one signaling field in two regions or two slices) and
   cross-field comparisons (two different signaling fields, or two genes).
5. Joint inference is primary, using excursion sets (R package =excursions=, Bolin and
   Lindgren); pointwise intervals are secondary, used only to show where a difference lies.
6. "Looks similar but differs" is reported with the size of the difference, never as a
   finding on its own.
7. A systematic version across all signaling fields runs on klone.
8. Signaling fields are primary; show one gene-field example as well.

* Two technical points the plan rests on

** Scaling, for cross-field comparisons

Two different scalings, doing different jobs:

- *Per unit of total counts* (decision 3). Divides each bin's value by that bin's total count.
  Removes variation in sequencing depth *across locations*. The model already provides this as
  =lambda=, the expected count per UMI.
- *By the field's own spatial average.* Divides a whole field by its average over the tissue.
  Removes the field's *overall level*, which for different genes or different ligand-receptor
  pairs mostly reflects capture efficiency and abundance. Concrete case: if field A is
  everywhere about 10 times field B, comparing raw values says "A is higher" at every
  location, which is true but uninformative. After dividing each by its own average, the
  comparison asks whether their *spatial patterns* differ.

Same-field comparisons need only the first. Cross-field comparisons need both: first the rate,
then each field divided by its own tissue average, computed within each posterior draw so the
uncertainty of the average is carried through.

Useful identity: both partners share the same total count at a bin, so
=min(N*a, N*b) = N*min(a, b)=. The rate-scale signaling field is the published count-scale
field divided by total counts; nothing about the operator changes.

** Why joint posterior draws are needed, and how to store them

Areal averages, slices and whole-field comparisons depend on how uncertainty is correlated
between locations, which per-bin summaries cannot give. The fitted models were never saved
(=02-array-run-model.R:733= is commented out), so genes must be refit with joint draws
(inlabru =generate()= on the fit, same model specification). A signaling field's posterior
comes from taking the minimum of a ligand draw and an independent receptor draw (the two genes
are fitted separately, so their posteriors are independent).

Storage: draws at every bin for every gene would be about 1,000 draws x 26,000 bins x 790
genes, roughly 160 GB as double precision (computed, not measured; 790 = genes needed by the
1,477 pairs). So, for the systematic run, store float32 draws only at the bins inside the
fixed regions and along the fixed slices, plus full-field draws for the few genes in the
showcase examples. Region averages of a signaling field must be computed from bin-level draws
of both partners, because the average of a minimum is not the minimum of averages.

* Comparators, and why they are needed

Our posterior intervals alone show what our method says. To show it changes conclusions, each
example also shows how people answer the same question without it:

1. *Eyeballing the point-estimate maps*, which is how signaling fields are usually read.
2. *A naive test treating bins as independent observations*: for example, compare the
   regional values bin by bin with a t-test or Wilcoxon test. Neighboring bins are strongly
   correlated (the fitted correlation length is about 10 bins), so this test acts as if it had
   far more independent data than it does and calls too many differences significant.

The core demonstration is a case where the map looks different and the naive test agrees, but
the joint posterior says the data do not support it. The reverse case is reported with its
size.
