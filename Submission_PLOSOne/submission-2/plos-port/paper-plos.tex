\section*{Abstract}
Maps of ligand-receptor interaction estimated from spatial transcriptomics are usually read by eye from point estimates, so that a field which looks higher in one region than another is taken to differ there. Such a reading ignores two things: the sampling uncertainty of the estimates, which for an interaction field combines the uncertainty of two genes, and the spatial correlation between neighboring locations, which leaves a region with far less independent information than its size suggests. We fit a Bayesian geostatistical model to the raw counts of each ligand and receptor gene in one field of view of an embryonic day 16.5 mouse embryo section profiled with Stereo-seq: a Poisson likelihood with the local total count as an offset and a Gaussian random field over space. Joint posterior draws from these fits give a posterior for each of 1,477 ligand-receptor interaction fields, and we use these posteriors to ask whether the differences visible on the maps are supported by the data, for averages over regions, along transects, and over whole fields, with joint inference by excursion sets as the primary answer. The regions, transects and example fields were fixed before any posterior comparison was computed. Of 19,201 comparisons between pairs of regions, 11,527 show a ratio of at least 1.25 on the point-estimate maps, and for 3,603 of these (31\%) the posterior 95\% interval for the ratio includes 1; the share is highest, about nine in ten, for small regions within one tissue. A smaller number of comparisons that look alike on the maps are credibly different, mostly by factors near the threshold. A rank test applied to the smoothed map values, treating locations as independent, finds a difference in 90\% of the comparisons, the joint posterior in 46\%, and the posterior with its spatial correlation removed in 73\%, so that most of the disagreement traces to the correlation rather than to the uncertainty at each location. Along transects and over whole fields, statements that hold jointly mark far fewer locations than statements made location by location. Clustering the same fields yields spatially coherent tissue domains that survive a spatial null and agree with independent anatomy well above chance. These results come from one field of view of one section on one platform, and the fields estimate the potential for signaling under the co-expression assumption standard in cell-cell communication analysis, not signaling itself.

\subsection*{Morphogenic field interactions are fundamental to tissue biology and regenerative medicine}

Cells within tissues are continuously processing information and updating their states, receiving cues from their local microenvironment and contributing cues to their local milieu. The study of cellular connectivity (the potential for information flow between cells) and cell-to-cell signaling (information flow between cells) is fundamental to the study of tissue biology. Accurate modeling of cellular connectivity and cell-to-cell signaling, particularly through ligand-receptor syntax, provides a quantitative foundation for understanding tissue-scale changes and dynamics (Fig~\ref{fig:proj_schema}).

It has long been understood that tissues are shaped by morphogenic signaling between cells \cite{Vainio1997,Basson2012,Hagolani2019,Wu2023}. During development, homeostasis, disease, and regenerative remodeling, cells within tissues release signaling molecules which diffuse through the extracellular matrix and can be locally received and processed by resident cells. Until recently, it has only been possible to capture data on a small number of morphogenic mechanisms at once from a single tissue. However, it is the \textit{holistic set} of soluble and insoluble cue intensities, taken together as a single combinatoric `input' \cite{Li2019,Klumpe2022,Granados2024}, that encodes the full spectrum of locally-transducable signaling information, often irreducible to the individual cues themselves \cite{Brennan2012,Ellison2016,Scuderi2025}. In other words, extracellular signaling is intrinsically high-dimensional (information is encoded via specific combinations of cues at particular levels, which are processed in parallel by resident cells) and is intrinsically spatially patterned: as an organism develops, intersecting 3-dimensional signaling gradients combine to drive tightly localized cellular differentiation, proliferation, and migration events. These morphogenic field interactions are major governors of tissue-scale emergent behavior including developmental morphogenesis and adult tissue inflammation \& homeostasis \cite{Weaver1997,Hironaka2012,Meizlish2021,Kicheva2023}. To understand tissue-scale change, therefore, we need robust tools to study high-dimensional signaling and connectivity dynamics in both time and space.

Spatialomics is newly presenting scientists with an opportunity to estimate proxies for thousands of morphogenic fields at once, at both the raw and relative information level, thereby allowing us to model and visualize patterns that are consistent with the extracellular signaling thought to control cellular differentiation and transition in living tissues. However, to properly handle and analyze these data, we need appropriately designed statistical approaches. This paper presents one such approach, and shows that by adapting techniques common in geospatial data analysis, we are able to model and visualize, with quantified uncertainty, the high-dimensional patterns of extracellular signaling that have long been theorized to be fundamental to tissue growth and change.

\subsection*{The critical need for spatial statistics in tissue biology}

Spatialomics data differ from the true state of the tissue in two important ways. First, regardless of platform resolution, all spatialomics data collection methods collect data at discrete locations, necessarily creating error in reported spatial sampling coordinates. Second, no bulk RNA, single cell, or spatialomics methods perform true censuses; that is, they do not measure every single molecule within every sample, and in many cases measure only a small fraction of the total present molecules \cite{Yue2023,Wang2023}. As a result, observed data from all spatial transcriptomic methods is subject to sampling variability. Ignoring sampling variability while performing data-analysis can lead to bias, over- or under-confidence in results, and false findings \cite{li2019a}. Properly accounting for the sampling variability in any dataset requires a statistical model aligned with the data-generating mechanism.

Our statistical goal in this work was to take spatialomics data observations as input and to produce output which estimates the true state of the tissue, acknowledging and accounting for the important ways in which the data differs from the tissue itself. That is, we need statistical models to take transcriptomic data measured at \textbf{discrete} spatial locations as input and to produce estimates of \textbf{continuous} spatial fields as outputs, along with estimates of uncertainty, which act as a statistical prediction of transcriptomic counts across the spatial observation domain. Spatial statistics has a rich literature of tools to leverage which are designed exactly for this purpose. Furthermore, they account for the spatial autocorrelation present within these datasets, which, if ignored, can again lead to incorrect uncertainty estimates and false findings \cite{cannon2001}.

\subsection*{Biological tissues are spatially structured}

Advances in genetics data collection methodologies have led to rich, beautiful datasets which can easily be mistaken to be ``the truth."  Spatialomics, loosely defined as the group of technologies capable of measuring molecular characteristics of tissues within their natural spatial alignment, has justifiably been heralded as a transformative frontier in life sciences. However, the quickly developing methods, and the immense amounts of data produced, have outpaced \textit{analysis techniques}, which need to mature \cite{bressan2023}.

Spatialomics collection methods have been developed precisely because there is observable and necessary spatial structure apparent in tissues. Yet, this fact isn't often explicitly incorporated in analyses. The spatial structure of healthy vs. diseased tissues is often radically dissimilar, but many currently-practiced computational methods are descendants of single cell techniques which were necessarily non-spatial. Ignoring the spatial organization, at best, may omit useful information and, at worst, may lead to an incorrect interpretation of the data. When approaching morphogenic field analysis, i.e., the quantitative study of high-dimensional extracellular signaling mediated by ligand-receptor binding interactions via the extracellular milieu, it is essential to take these principles into account, or we risk misinterpreting statistically spurious findings.

\subsection*{Reading interaction fields under uncertainty}

The two preceding points, sampling variability and spatial correlation, bear directly on how maps of ligand-receptor interaction are read in practice. Such maps are usually interpreted by eye: a field that looks higher in one region than another is taken to differ there, and a band that stands out along a structure is taken to be real. Two features of the data make that reading less reliable than it appears. First, each estimate carries sampling uncertainty, and for an interaction field, which is built from two genes, the uncertainty of both partners is inherited. Second, estimates at neighboring locations are correlated, so a region containing hundreds of locations may carry only a handful of independent pieces of information about the field's level there. Tests that treat locations as independent observations have been shown in simulation to overstate the evidence in exactly this situation \cite{vasconcelos2026smide,seal2026spacebf}. A posterior distribution over whole fields addresses both features at once, and it permits statements about many locations simultaneously, such as the largest set of locations at which a field exceeds a reference with a stated joint probability \cite{bolin2015excursion}. Joint statements of this kind have been used to map activation in functional brain imaging with the same modeling tools we use \cite{mejia2020bayesian}, and simultaneous credible bands built from joint posterior draws have summarized interaction curves between cell types in multiplexed imaging \cite{eliason2026shade}. In spatial transcriptomics, Bayesian models have been used to compare gene expression between anatomical regions \cite{aijo2019splotch} and to place credible intervals on ligand-receptor co-expression at each location \cite{seal2026spacebf}. We are not aware of work that uses joint posterior statements to compare ligand-receptor interaction fields between regions, along transects and over whole fields, which is what we do here.

In this manuscript we fit a continuous spatial model to the raw counts of each ligand and receptor gene in one field of view of a mouse embryo section, obtaining a posterior for every gene field and, from the two partners' posteriors, a posterior for every ligand-receptor interaction field. Our central question is whether the differences that are visible on the point-estimate maps of these fields are supported by the data once that posterior is taken into account. We ask it in three forms, for the ratio of two regional averages, for the profile of a field along a transect, and for a whole field against a reference, and in each form the primary answer is a joint posterior statement. Beside each answer we set the readings that are usually made without a posterior: the map itself, read with an explicit threshold, and a rank test that treats the locations of the map as independent observations. To make the comparison fair, the regions, transects and example fields were all fixed before any posterior comparison was computed, and the frozen design is provided with the code. We also report, more briefly than in our original submission, that clustering the full collection of interaction fields yields spatially coherent tissue domains, together with the controls that bound what those domains show. Throughout, the demonstration is on one field of view of one section of one embryo, and the fields estimate the potential for signaling under the co-expression assumption, not signaling itself.

\section*{Materials and methods}\label{sec:materials}

\subsection*{Overview}

\subsubsection*{Principles of ligand-receptor analysis}

Our focus in this paper was to model a useful proxy for local ligand-receptor binding \textit{density}. This is different from local ligand-receptor connectivity \textit{character}, which is the primary focus of many cell-to-cell communication inference tools including our prior work \cite{Raredon2022}. This distinction is non-trivial. When ligand-receptor connectivity \textit{character} is computed, individual feature values represent a proxy for \textit{relative} signal strength; when ligand-receptor binding \textit{density} is computed, individual feature values represent a proxy for \textit{absolute} signal strength. Our focus on binding density in this paper, rather than character, is what makes the outputs suitable for propagation to downstream models, since an absolute quantity can be carried forward without the reference-dependence that a relative one introduces. By applying spatial statistics to the raw counts in spatial transcriptomics, rather than the normalized counts, our model outputs can be treated as a distinct layer of information for multi-modal data analysis, one that estimates a proxy for local ligand-receptor binding density within the tissue. This output can then be normalized, similarly to how we usually normalize local gene counts to relative values, to yield proxies for local ligand-receptor connectivity \textit{character} more familiar to most in the field.

All connectivity tools require us to convolve information from the ligand field with information from the receptor field. Historically, this has been done by computing either the product \cite{Raredon2022} or the geometric mean \cite{Wilk2024} of all subunits involved. Here, we tested alternative approaches to convolving the ligand and the receptor values. Assuming that the counts of individual transcripts are proportional to the counts of individual proteins (an almost certainly \textit{incorrect} assumption, but one that we are forced to accept at this time) we may treat ligand-receptor binding as a stoichiometric process, i.e., a function of the concentration of the ligand and the concentration of the receptor.

Ligands diffuse locally, with a characteristic diffusion distance roughly proportional to the molecular weight and chemical character of the molecular species and how it interacts with other matrix elements. Extracellular ligands can be sensed and processed by tissue-resident cells expressing specific receptor profiles. The computational approaches we are aware of address this fundamental property of tissue biology by first creating local neighborhoods in histologic space and then computing cell-to-cell ligand-receptor connectivity within each neighborhood. When working with continuous field models, as is demonstrated here, local neighborhood construction is no longer necessary, because the spatial field, intrinsically, can be considered as a filter which takes in noisy spatial data and returns a smoothed version. Although the smoothed output is technically an estimate of the underlying state of the tissue, in practice the smoothing process can be viewed as sharing locally-measured information between nearby areas. This modeling approach thereby provides a framework for convolution of ligand and receptor fields. The fields themselves can be independently tuned to reflect specific molecular properties, and the convolution approach customized to reflect binding kinetics.

\subsubsection*{Principles of spatial modeling}

Spatial statistics is a field of statistics used to study datasets in which the location of the data observations is recorded. Typically, a spatial statistical model will explicitly account for spatial dependence among observations to acknowledge that spatially nearby observations are often more strongly correlated than observations that are located further apart. The purpose of most statistical models is to use imperfect data to provide an estimate of a population-level target of interest and the associated uncertainty of that estimate (from which statistical inference, e.g. confidence intervals or hypothesis tests can be calculated and performed). Leveraging spatial correlation when it exists can result in more precise estimates and ignoring it when it exists leads to incorrect (typically overly confident) uncertainty estimates \cite{valcu2010}. Non-probabilistic interpolation techniques cannot account for the sampling variability or produce reliable uncertainty estimates. In this work, we demonstrate a spatial statistical model that can be used to estimate biological fields across the tissue from which the spatialomics data sample was collected. The estimated fields represent the result that would be expected if the experiment were repeated many times.

One of the primary decisions to make when performing a spatial statistical analysis is whether the spatial domain (and thus the model) should be discrete or continuous in nature. Despite that many of the contemporary spatialomics data collection tools generate data on discrete ``spot" arrays, the underlying tissue- and cellular-level \textit{processes}, which are the target of our study, are not constrained to occur only at data observation locations (spots) or any finite set of locations.

For this reason, we have chosen to pursue a continuous spatial modeling approach. This does not mean that we believe there to be continuous distributions of discrete morphogen molecules at all points in space (we know this is not true), but rather that our estimate shows at each point in space the average values, and that spread of the values, that we would expect to see if we could repeat the experiment (of performing spatialomics data collection on a tissue slice) many times. Our goal with the spatial statistical models is two-fold: 1) to estimate the unknown underlying population field while 2) acknowledging the uncertainty in our estimate due to the sampling variability present in the observed dataset.

\subsubsection*{A general approach to spatial hierarchical modeling}

We formulate our model in a generalizable Bayesian hierarchical modeling framework that can generally be thought of as having three stages:
\begin{alignat*}{2}
&\mbox{Stage 1, data generation:} &\mbox{Data } &| \mbox{ Process}\\
&\mbox{Stage 2, the (latent) process: }  &\mbox{Process } &| \mbox{ Hyperparameters }\\
&\mbox{Stage 3: hyperparameters }  &\mbox{Hyperparameters } &| \mbox{ Priors}
\end{alignat*}

This broad framework is broadly applicable to many data collection settings where 1) data observations, which depend on some underlying process, are collected, 2) the underlying process, called the latent process since it cannot be directly observed, can be represented as a probability distribution whose parameters can be inferred from the data observations, and 3) the latent process itself may depend on some additional parameters. This simple yet powerful framework is well-established and a useful pedagogical tool for describing hierarchical modeling \cite{cressie2011statistics}. To make this concrete in the spatialomics setting, we could define the following three stages as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item mRNA is collected across a tissue using a spatial transcriptomics data collection technique. At each location where data is collected, only a subset of all possible mRNA at that location is collected. Furthermore, the actual levels of mRNA at any particular location stochastically vary as mRNA is created and destroyed in accordance with the unobserved cell state at and around that location. The latent cellular state cannot directly be observed, but we aim to infer something about cell state using the observable mRNA counts.
    \item The cell state, the latent process, is also a stochastic process. Conditional on the cell type, cell size, cellular microenvironment and signaling networks a cell is involved in, the state of the cell follows a probabilistic distribution. The state of the cell and the previously listed cell characteristics drive the stochastic mRNA production discussed in the above description of the data collection stage.
    \item To complete the Bayesian specification, some of the values that the cell state depends upon (e.g. the microenvironment of a cell) can also be viewed as stochastic processes whose parameters can be specified with prior distributions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{A general spatial statistical model for spatialomics}

The geostatistical model described in \eqref{eq:one-feat-model} is applicable to spatialomics datasets that report counts at a set of spatial locations together with a measure of local sampling effort; the conditions under which we expect it to transfer to other platforms are set out in the \nameref{sec:discussion} section. Unlike many contemporary spatialomics and genomics data analyses which use some version of standardized (``normalized'') counts, we have proposed to directly model the ``raw'' counts from the spatialomics platform. This allows us to work with a generative model: the proposed model can be viewed as one capable of generating the data that is actually observed and collected when one uses a spatialomics platform.

Let $\bs\in\mathcal{S}$ denote a spatial location over the continuous spatial domain $\mathcal{S}$, which here is the two-dimensional field of view of the tissue section. We write $F_i(\bs)$ for the count of the $i^{th}$ feature (a single ligand or receptor gene) at location $\bs$, treated as a random variable, and $y_i(\bs)$ for an observed realization of it. We write $\N(\bs)$ for the total counts at $\bs$, that is, the number of unique molecular identifiers (nUMI) recovered at that location.

To explicitly account for the differences between the raw counts and standardized counts, we specify the \textit{expected} counts from feature $i$ as the product of the total counts, $\N(\bs)$, and a rate parameter, $\lambda_i(\bs)$, which represents the expected counts of the $i^{th}$ feature per unit of total counts at location $\bs$. That is, $\E\big(F_i(\bs)\,\big|\,\N(\bs), \lambda_i(\bs)\big) = \N(\bs)\times \lambda_i(\bs)$, so that $\N(\bs)$ enters as an exposure term and $\lambda_i(\bs)$ carries the spatial signal for feature $i$. The conditioning matters: $\lambda_i(\bs)$ is itself a random field, and $\N(\bs)$ may be treated either as random or as fixed at its observed value, so this is the conditional mean of the data stage rather than a claim about the marginal expectation of $F_i$.

The general hierarchical model then takes the form:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:one-feat-model}
\begin{alignedat}{999}
& \mbox{Data stage} & F_i(\bs) & \, | \, \lambda_i(\bs), \N(\bs) &&\sim \mbox{Poisson}\big( \N(\bs)\times\lambda_i(\bs)\big)\\
& & \N(\bs) & \, | \, \lambda_N(\bs) &&\sim \mbox{Poisson}\big( \lambda_N(\bs)\big)\\
&&&&&\\
&\mbox{Process stage} \ \ \ \ \ & \mbox{log}\,\lambda_i(\bs)& = \alpha_i + u_i(\bs), \ \ u_i(\cdot)\,|\,\btheta_i &&\sim GP\big(0, \Sigma(\btheta_i)\big)\\
&\ \ \ \ \ & \mbox{log}\,\lambda_N(\bs)& = \alpha_N + u_N(\bs), \ \ u_N(\cdot)\,|\,\btheta_N &&\sim GP\big(0, \Sigma(\btheta_N)\big)\\
&&&&&\\
&\mbox{Prior stage} & \bgamma_i \equiv (\alpha_i,\btheta_i), \ \ \bgamma_N &\equiv (\alpha_N,\btheta_N) &&\sim \pi_\gamma.
\end{alignedat}
\end{equation}

Each symbol is defined as follows. In the data stage, the counts of feature $i$ at location $\bs$ are Poisson with mean equal to the total counts at $\bs$ multiplied by the rate $\lambda_i(\bs)$. In the process stage, $\log\lambda_i(\bs)$ is decomposed into a feature-specific scalar intercept, $\alpha_i$, which sets the overall level of feature $i$ across the field of view, and $u_i(\bs)$, a zero-mean Gaussian process over $\mathcal{S}$ which carries all spatial variation in the rate. The notation $GP\big(0,\Sigma(\btheta_i)\big)$ means that any finite collection of values of $u_i$ is multivariate Gaussian with mean zero and covariance matrix $\Sigma(\btheta_i)$, whose $(\bs,\bs')$ entry is a Mat\'ern covariance function evaluated at the distance between $\bs$ and $\bs'$. The vector $\btheta_i$ collects the two parameters of that covariance function for feature $i$: the range $\rho_i$, which is the distance at which the correlation between two locations has decayed to approximately $0.13$, and the marginal standard deviation $\sigma_i$, which is the standard deviation of $u_i(\bs)$ at any single location. We do not estimate the Mat\'ern smoothness. In the stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) representation described below, we fix the order of the operator at 2 (the \texttt{alpha = 2} setting in the implementation), which corresponds to a Mat\'ern smoothness of $\nu=1$ in two spatial dimensions, so that $\btheta_i = (\rho_i, \sigma_i)$ and nothing further. Note that this order parameter is unrelated to the intercepts $\alpha_i$ and $\alpha_N$, and we avoid writing it as a symbol for that reason. The symbols $\lambda_N(\bs)$, $\alpha_N$, $u_N(\bs)$, $\btheta_N = (\rho_N,\sigma_N)$ and $\Sigma(\btheta_N)$ are the corresponding objects for the latent model of the total counts. The Bayesian specification is completed by the hyperprior $\pi_\gamma$. Note that $\bgamma_i$ is feature-specific, one per modeled feature, whereas $\bgamma_N$ belongs to the single total-count model and is shared across features; the original manuscript collected all four into one feature-subscripted vector, which was not correct.

\paragraph*{The status of the latent total-count model.} The second line of the data stage, and with it every symbol carrying an $N$ subscript, specifies a \textit{latent} model for the total counts. We retain it in \eqref{eq:one-feat-model} because it is the general form of the framework and because we did fit it; the per-feature outputs of those joint fits are the panels in S3 File. However, no result reported in this manuscript uses it. In every fit reported in the figures and in Table~\ref{tab:lik-comp}, $\N(\bs)$ is the \textit{observed} nUMI at location $\bs$, entered as a fixed and known offset rather than modeled, so the reported model is the first data-stage line together with the first process-stage line alone. We should be clear about what using the observed nUMI as an offset does and does not assume. It is not a claim that $\N(\bs)$ is a deterministic quantity. The total counts at a location are themselves the outcome of a stochastic process, both in the underlying transcriptional activity of the cells sampled there and in the capture and sequencing that subsample it. Using the observed total as an offset means \textit{conditioning} on the realized value rather than modeling the process that produced it, which is a modeling choice, not a statement about the physics.

We have quantified what that choice costs, comparing the 23 features fitted under both specifications. The fitted feature fields agree closely in spatial pattern: the Pearson correlation between the joint and offset fits ranges from 0.986 to 0.997, with a median of 0.995. They differ modestly in level, the mean absolute difference between the two fitted fields being 5.1\% to 13.5\% of the mean fitted count for that feature, with a median of 6.6\%. The posterior \textit{uncertainty} is essentially unchanged: the ratio of posterior standard deviations between the two fits has a median of 0.998 across features, and the ratio of 95\% credible interval widths a median of 0.965, with the joint model giving wider intervals for 10 of the 23 features and narrower for 13. Modeling $\N(\bs)$ as latent therefore neither propagated appreciable extra uncertainty into the feature fields nor materially sharpened them.

We adopted the offset specification for the full feature set on that basis: it preserves the spatial structure the downstream analyses use and carries the same uncertainty, while being simpler and substantially cheaper to fit. We would not claim the two specifications are interchangeable for a question about absolute magnitude, and we would expect the joint model to matter more at lower sequencing depth, where the observed total is a noisier summary of local transcriptional activity than it is here.

\paragraph*{Priors.} We set independent wide Gaussian priors on the intercepts $\alpha_i$ and $\alpha_N$. On the Mat\'ern parameters we place the joint penalized complexity prior of Fuglstad et al.\ \cite{fuglstad2019}, which is specified by one tail probability for each component. For the analyses reported here we set $P(\rho_i < 500) = 0.95$ and $P(\sigma_i > 0.1) = 0.05$, identically for every feature $i$, with the range expressed in the raw coordinate units of the Stereo-seq chip. Five hundred coordinate units is ten bins at the bin-50 resolution used throughout, and about one twentieth of the width of the field of view, so the range prior places most of its mass on fields that vary over distances well short of the field of view, and the marginal standard deviation prior shrinks $u_i$ toward a constant field unless the data indicate otherwise. A sum-to-zero constraint is imposed on each fitted field so that $\alpha_i$ and $u_i(\bs)$ are separately identifiable.

\paragraph*{From the model to the implementation.} The Gaussian processes are not represented by a dense covariance matrix. We use the SPDE representation \cite{lindgren2011}, in which a Mat\'ern field is approximated by a Gaussian Markov random field defined on the nodes of a triangulated mesh over $\mathcal{S}$; $\Sigma(\btheta_i)$ is therefore realized in the code as a sparse precision matrix indexed by mesh nodes rather than by observation locations. The mesh used for the reported analyses has approximately 10{,}200 nodes covering the 26{,}000 observation bins. Inference is by integrated nested Laplace approximation \cite{rue2009}, using the \texttt{R-INLA} and \texttt{inlabru} packages, and posterior summaries and predictive scores are computed from 1{,}000 posterior samples per feature. Because the correspondence between the notation above and the analysis code is not otherwise obvious, S9 Table gives a row for each mathematical object in \eqref{eq:one-feat-model}, naming the script and the code object that implements it.

Due to the large number zeros often present in transcriptomics datasets, we also tested variations on the modeling framework wherein we replaced the Poisson likelihood for the $i^{th}$ feature with either a zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP) process or a zero-adjusted Poisson (ZAP) process. Both of these alternatives allow for a spatially varying probability of observing no counts. In these variants, the data generating mechanism can then viewed as first flipping a coin to determine if any counts from feature $i$ will be observed, followed by a data observation from a Poisson process if the coin flip succeeds. Additional detail can be found in the \nameref{sec:meth-model-comp} section.

\subsubsection*{Quantification of categorical spatial structure}
\label{sec:elsa}

To assess the impact of our modeling choices, we desired a way of quantifying the spatial coherence of our final output clusters, represented as a categorical variable. To this end, we employed ELSA (entropy-based local indicator of spatial association), which is applicable to categorical variables and does not depend on the number of categories \cite{naimi2019elsa}. ELSA is calculated by multiplying a spatially localized (scaled) Shannon entropy calculation with a localized measure of spatial dissimilarity to produce a measure of spatial association. Each of these components are bounded between 0 and 1, ensuring that ELSA is also bounded between 0 and 1. Locations with high values of ELSA indicate spatial regions where the variable of interest is less structured and spatially disparate while low values of ELSA suggest higher levels of spatial coherence within the local neighborhood.

\subsubsection*{Dataset selection}
To demonstrate our approach in detail, we use data from Chen et al.\ 2022 \cite{Chen2022}, the Mouse Organogenesis Spatiotemporal Transcriptomic Atlas (MOSTA), a spatial time-course atlas of mouse embryogenesis acquired with the spatial transcriptomics platform Stereo-seq. The material analyzed here is therefore a mouse embryo section drawn from a public atlas. No new tissue was collected for this study, no human or patient material is involved, and we did not perform the tissue processing or sequencing ourselves; readers should consult the original publication for the experimental protocol. We selected this resource because it is publicly available, information-rich, straightforward to interpret anatomically, and relevant to both developmental and regenerative biology.

Stereo-seq captures polyadenylated RNA on a chip patterned with DNA nanoball spots, each bearing capture probes that carry a coordinate identity barcode encoding the spot's position, a unique molecular identifier, and a poly-T tail. Spots are approximately 220 nm across, with a center-to-center spacing of either 500 or 715 nm depending on the chip variant; the source publication does not state which variant carried the embryo sections, so we report positions below in spot units rather than converting them to physical distances. Because the spacing is far finer than a cell, individual spots are very sparse, and counts are conventionally aggregated into square bins before analysis. We follow that practice.

From this atlas we selected a single section from embryonic day 16.5, section identifier \texttt{E16.5\_E1S3}, and within it a single field of view spanning $x$ from 15000 to 25000 and $y$ from 11500 to 18000 in raw chip coordinates (Fig~\ref{fig:data_param}). One unit of those coordinates is one nanoball spot, so the field of view is 10,000 by 6,500 spots; its size in millimetres depends on which chip variant was used, which the source publication does not specify. The field of view was chosen to be visually recognizable to both experts and non-specialists and to contain several distinct organs, allowing close study of morphogenic field interactions during development. At the bin size used throughout (bin 50, meaning a $50\times50$ block of capture spots), this field of view comprises 26,000 spatial locations, each of which is an observation in the models below.

\subsubsection*{Dataset preprocessing}

Raw count data were loaded into \texttt{R} and restricted to the field of view described above. We then filtered the transcriptomic features to only those measured within this boundary and, following the workflow from the original publication, collapsed the ultra-high resolution, sparse data into square spatial bins at three sizes (10, 25, and 50). All results shown in the figures of this manuscript use the bin-50 data, which divides the field of view into a $200\times130$ grid of 26,000 bins; S1 File documents this raw-to-bin step and its outputs. Bin 50 was chosen as the coarsest of the three, and therefore the least sparse per observation; the choice of binning is a modeling decision rather than a property of the platform, and results at other bin sizes are not reported here.

To identify ligand and receptor genes, we cross-referenced the mouse version of the FANTOM5 database \cite{Ramilowski2015} against the feature names of the binned data, yielding 974 distinct genes that were either ligands or receptors. We further refined this gene list to only include ligands or receptors showing at least two counts in at least one bin, and at least one-quarter-of-one-percent of the bins in the field of view expressing the gene at any non-zero level (S2 File shows the visual outputs which were used to decide this threshold). This final step of feature selection yielded a curated list of 918 individual ligand or receptor genes.

\subsubsection*{Dataset modeling}\label{sec:meth-models}

We individually modeled the spatial field of the counts of each curated feature using a spatial Gaussian process model with Poisson observations, as defined in \eqref{eq:one-feat-model}, with the observed nUMI at each bin as a fixed offset. The models were fit using \texttt{R-INLA} and \texttt{inlabru}, and predictions from these models were then used in downstream analyses.

Not every curated feature yielded a usable field. Of the 918 genes on the curated list, three (\textit{a}, \textit{Calcb} and \textit{Cckar}) were excluded before fitting, and three further genes (\textit{Fgf1}, \textit{Fzd7} and \textit{Trhr}) did not produce a converged fit. All downstream analyses therefore use the 912 features with fitted spatial fields. Of the FANTOM5 ligand-receptor pairings, 1477 have both partners among these 912 features, and these 1477 pairs define the interaction fields analyzed in the remainder of the manuscript. They are not 1477 numerically distinct fields. The common-minimum operator described below returns whichever of the two partner fields is smaller, so pairs that share a partner which is the smaller of the two at every location yield identical fields; seven pairs of the ligand GDF9 with different receptors, for example, give a single field. The 1477 pairs yield 1449 numerically distinct fields under that operator.

\subsubsection*{Comparison of 3 data models}\label{sec:meth-model-comp}

We experimented with three different likelihood models: a Poisson likelihood, a zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP) likelihood, and a zero-adjusted Poisson likelihood (ZAP). The ZIP and ZAP models provide extra flexibility to acknowledge that the raw count data is often very sparse, with many spots returning a measurement of 0 for any particular feature:

Writing $\mu_i(\bs) = \N(\bs)\,\lambda_i(\bs)$ for the Poisson mean of \eqref{eq:one-feat-model}, so that the observed total counts enter as an exposure term in all three cases, the likelihoods are

\begin{equation}\label{eq:three-lik}
\begin{aligned}
    \mbox{Poisson:}\  &P\left(F_i(\bs)=y\ |\ \mu_i(\bs)\right) &&= \mbox{Poisson}\big(y\ |\ \mu_i(\bs)\big)\\
    \mbox{ZIP:}\  &P\left(F_i(\bs) = y\ |\ \mu_i(\bs), p_i(\bs)\right) &&= p_i(\bs)\mathcal{I}_{y=0}  + (1-p_i(\bs))\mbox{Poisson}\big(y\ |\  \mu_i(\bs)\big) \\
    \mbox{ZAP:}\  &P\left(F_i(\bs)= y\ |\ \mu_i(\bs), p_i(\bs)\right) &&= p_i(\bs)\mathcal{I}_{y=0}  + (1-p_i(\bs)) \mbox{Poisson}_{>0}\big(y\ |\  \mu_i(\bs)\big),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $p_i(\bs)$ is a spatially varying probability of a 0 observation for feature $i$, which is modeled with its own spatial Gaussian random field using a logit link, $\mbox{Poisson}_{>0}$ denotes the zero-truncated Poisson, and $\mathcal{I}_{y=0}$ is an indicator function which takes a value of 1 if $y=0$ and takes a value of 0 otherwise. The ZIP and ZAP models differ in that the probability of a 0 in the ZAP model is directly governed by $p_i(\bs)$ and allows for the probability of a 0 to be less than a standard Poisson, whereas the ZIP models can only increase the probability of a 0. ZAP models are typically selected when the process for a 0 occurrence differs substantially from the process driving the non-0 values.

At each observed location, $\bs_j$, model performance was assessed by comparing the observed count of feature $i$ at that location, $y_i(\bs_j)$, against the posterior predictive distribution of $F_i(\bs_j)$. Writing $\E(\cdot|\mbox{data})$, $\v(\cdot|\mbox{data})$ and $\mbox{med}(\cdot|\mbox{data})$ for the mean, variance and median of that posterior predictive distribution, the four location-level scores we used are
\begin{align*}
    AE_i(\bs_j)&=\left|y_i(\bs_j) - \mbox{med}\big(F_i(\bs_j)|\mbox{data}\big)\right|\\
    SE_i(\bs_j)&=\left(y_i(\bs_j)-  \E\big(F_i(\bs_j)|\mbox{data}\big)\right)^2\\
    DS_i(\bs_j)  &= \frac{\left(y_i(\bs_j) - \E\big(F_i(\bs_j)|\mbox{data}\big)\right)^2}{\v\big(F_i(\bs_j)|\mbox{data}\big)} + \log\left(\v\big(F_i(\bs_j)|\mbox{data}\big)\right)\\
    LS_i(\bs_j)&=-\log\left(P\big(F_i(\bs_j)=y_i(\bs_j)|\mbox{data}\big)\right).
\end{align*}

The absolute error (AE) and the squared error (SE) are point-forecast measures: they compare a single summary of the predictive distribution against the observation and ignore its spread. Where we are free to choose that summary we use the posterior predictive median, because it is the more stable summary for the skewed, low-count predictive distributions that arise here, and because it is the summary that minimizes expected absolute error. The squared error and the Dawid-Sebastiani score are defined in terms of the predictive mean and variance, so for those we use the mean, as their definitions require. This is why AE alone is computed against the median. The Dawid-Sebastiani score (DS) is a proper scoring rule combining the predictive mean and variance, and the (negated) logarithmic score (LS) is a strictly proper scoring rule which uses the entire predictive distribution \cite{gneiting2007strictly}. All four are negatively oriented, so that a smaller value indicates better performance; note that DS may be negative, because it contains a log-variance term, so ``smaller'' means more negative for that score.

These location-level scores are reduced to the four per-feature quantities reported in Table~\ref{tab:lik-comp}. For feature $i$, the mean absolute error is $MAE_i = \mbox{mean}_j\, AE_i(\bs_j)$, the root mean squared error is $RMSE_i = \sqrt{\mbox{mean}_j\, SE_i(\bs_j)}$, the mean Dawid-Sebastiani score is $MDS_i = \mbox{mean}_j\, DS_i(\bs_j)$, and the mean log score is $MLG_i = \mbox{mean}_j\, LS_i(\bs_j)$, each averaged over the observation locations $\bs_j$. Table~\ref{tab:lik-comp} then reports the average of each of these across features.

\subsubsection*{Quantitative optimization of ligand-receptor convolution}
\label{sec:meth-conv-comp}

We experimented with multiple different approaches to ligand-receptor convolution. In all approaches studied, we did not consider ligand nor receptor sequestration due to binding, nor did we consider cross-mechanism inhibition or competitive interference, which can be the focus of future studies. We tested product, geometric mean, and a new approach labeled `common minimum convolution', which computes an upper bound on the number of local binding events that the estimated ligand and receptor levels would imply, under the assumption that all ligand and all receptor bind to one another in a 1:1 fashion. As with every quantity in this paper, that bound is model-derived from transcript counts rather than measured. We also explored a prototype approach based on chemical kinetics, using the rate equation with a tunable dissociation constant to model local steady-state ratio between bound and unbound ligand.

Our goal was to learn which methods yielded the most fine-grained and spatially organized clusters by looking for methods that maximized the number of clusters while minimizing the spatial entropy as quantified by ELSA (see the \nameref{sec:elsa} section). We therefore performed a targeted optimization experiment to identify which combination of parameters, models, and data inputs, all else being equal, resolved the largest number of distinct local microenvironments while minimizing inter-cluster spatial entropy. ELSA was calculated locally and averaged across the modeling region to produce a single comprehensive score. This provided a means to visualize the boundaries between clusters, assessing how well they segregate in space, and to rank each computational approach by both number of clusters identified (all other parameters being equal) and the global spatial entropy of cluster segregation.

\subsubsection*{Quantifying the relationship between spatial structure and hyperfield dimensionality}

We use \textit{hyperfield} throughout for the full collection of inferred ligand-receptor interaction fields treated as a single high-dimensional object, and \textit{hyperfield dimensionality} for the number of those fields entering an analysis at once.

We used the results from a specific optimized workflow (Raw Counts - Poisson Model - Common Minimum Convolution) to examine how spatial domain architecture varies with the dimensionality of the signaling hyperfield, meaning the number of inferred interaction fields entering the clustering at once. We did this because contemporary biologic theory posits that the complex spatial domains observed in tissues are likely a multivariable function of high-dimensional signal combinations rather than of a small set of select mechanisms. We therefore performed an experiment in which we iteratively increased the number of individual features considered for latent-space embedding and clustering, holding the rest of the workflow fixed. This sweep varies one quantity, the feature count, and so characterizes that one dependence rather than the relationship in full. Individual ligand-receptor mechanism features were first ranked based on system-scale variance, and then a for-loop was written to embed and cluster the data, with otherwise fixed algorithmic parameters, using increasingly many ligand-receptor features (10, 50, 100, and then in steps of 50, up to all 1477 pairs). The input to this sweep inadvertently contained a second copy of four interaction fields (PDGFC and PDGFD each paired with PDGFRA and PDGFRB), read in from duplicated input files, so its full-depth clustering used 1481 columns. We expect the effect to be small, since four duplicated columns are scaled alongside more than a thousand others, but we have not measured it. Ligand-receptor connectivity field data were scaled (though, importantly, not sum-normalized first) and processed without any principal-component analysis. Latent-space neighborhood graph construction and clustering parameters were held constant to allow isolation of the effect of feature number. ELSA was run in all instances, with a scan across the local distance parameter. All outputs from each step were stored for downstream visualization and analysis.

Because a variance-ordered sweep admits the same fixed set of features at each depth, it confounds the number of fields entering the clustering with which fields they are. To separate the two, we repeated the sweep admitting uniformly random subsets of the 1449 distinct fields, at depths of 10, 50, 150, 350, 650 and 1000 fields, with 19 independent random subsets at each depth (18 at depth 50, where one run failed for a technical reason unrelated to the data), using the same scaling, neighbor-graph and clustering steps and parameters, and scoring each clustering by mean ELSA at radius 50.

\subsection*{Uncertainty-aware comparison of interaction fields}
\label{sec:meth-uq}

The analyses in this subsection ask whether differences between interaction fields, or between parts of one interaction field, are supported by the data once the posterior uncertainty of the fields is taken into account. We describe, in order, how joint posterior draws were obtained for every gene field and every interaction field; the regions and transects over which fields are compared; the quantities compared; the inferential statements made about them; the three readings that do not use the posterior, against which each statement is set; and how the example fields were chosen. All of this was specified, and the regions, transects and examples fixed, before any posterior comparison was computed. The frozen design, with a dated log of every later addition, is provided with the code (see \nameref{sec:code}).

\subsubsection*{Joint posterior draws}
\label{sec:meth-draws}

A comparison over a region, a transect or a whole field depends on how the posterior uncertainty is correlated between locations, and that correlation is not recorded in the per-bin posterior summaries from which the published fields were built. The fitted model objects behind those summaries had not been stored, so we refitted every gene needed by the 1,477 pairs (790 genes) under the specification of the \nameref{sec:meth-models} section, with the same data, mesh, priors, offset and likelihood, and from each fit drew 1,000 samples from the joint posterior of $\lambda_i(\bs)$ at all 26,000 bins, using the same sampler that produced the published summaries. Before using a refit we checked that it reproduced the stored per-bin posterior medians and 95\% interval widths of the published fit to within Monte Carlo error. It did so for 778 of the 790 genes. For the remaining 12, all sparsely detected, the refit differed by more than Monte Carlo error, and we could not establish why: the software versions used for the published fits were not recorded, and the refits used \texttt{R-INLA} 25.10.19 with \texttt{inlabru} 2.13.0 and 2.14.1. These 12 genes enter 17 of the 1,477 pairs. We flag those pairs rather than exclude them, and every count reported below is also given without them (S13 Table). We also checked that 1,000 draws suffice for the intervals we report: recomputing the 91 regional intervals of the example pairs on the first and second 500 draws separately moved an interval endpoint by a median of 0.025 on the log scale (4.5\% of the interval width; at most 0.27), and changed one credible call in 91.

\subsubsection*{Posterior of an interaction field}

All comparisons are made on the rate scale, so that differences in sequencing depth between locations cannot masquerade as differences in the field. For a ligand-receptor pair with ligand $L$ and receptor $R$, the interaction field on the rate scale is $M(\bs) = \min\big(\lambda_L(\bs), \lambda_R(\bs)\big)$. Because both partners share the total count $\N(\bs)$ at each bin, this is simply the published count-scale field divided by $\N(\bs)$, and nothing about the common-minimum operator changes. The ligand and the receptor are fitted separately, so their posteriors are independent, and a posterior draw of $M$ is formed bin by bin from one draw of each partner, $M^{(d)}(\bs) = \min\big(\lambda_L^{(d)}(\bs), \lambda_R^{(d)}(\bs)\big)$ for $d = 1,\dots,1000$. Any summary of $M$ over several bins, such as a regional average, is computed within each draw from these bin-level values and only then summarized across draws, because the average of a minimum is not the minimum of the averages.

\subsubsection*{Regions and transects}

All regions and transects were chosen from the published anatomical annotation and the total-count map alone, before any interaction field or posterior quantity was examined; they are drawn in Fig~\ref{fig:regions}A and S11 Fig. We index bins by $i = (x - 15025)/50 + 1 \in \{1,\dots,200\}$ and $j = (y - 11525)/50 + 1 \in \{1,\dots,130\}$. There are two kinds of region. The first are five fixed boxes of $15 \times 15$ bins (225 bins each), two in the heart, one in the liver and two in the spinal cord, each lying almost entirely within one annotated tissue; the two boxes within one tissue give comparisons in which we would expect a field to look alike. The second are five anatomical regions, each the set of all bins carrying one label in the published annotation of this section \cite{Chen2022}: heart (4,240 bins), liver (3,346), lung (1,785), spinal cord (2,953) and thymus (548). These annotations were produced by the atlas authors, not by our clustering, so they are independent of the fields being compared. We compare regions in 13 fixed pairs: the two heart boxes, the two spinal-cord boxes, the first heart box against the liver box, and all ten pairs of anatomical regions. We also fixed four straight transects, one bin per unit step: from the spinal cord out to muscle (51 bins), from the heart into the liver (116 bins), along the spinal cord (141 bins), and from the thymus into the heart (101 bins).

\subsubsection*{Quantities compared}

Every comparison is formed as a log ratio within each posterior draw and reported as a ratio. For one interaction field and two regions $A$ and $B$, the contrast in draw $d$ is $\log \bar{M}^{(d)}_A - \log \bar{M}^{(d)}_B$, where $\bar{M}^{(d)}_A$ denotes the average of $M^{(d)}(\bs)$ over the bins of $A$. Two different interaction fields $M_1$ and $M_2$ differ in overall level for reasons that have more to do with abundance and capture efficiency than with spatial pattern, so before comparing them we divide each by its own average over the whole field of view, within each draw, $S^{(d)}(\bs) = M^{(d)}(\bs) / \bar{M}^{(d)}$, which carries the uncertainty of that average through; the cross-field contrast in region $A$ is then $\log \bar{S}^{(d)}_{1,A} - \log \bar{S}^{(d)}_{2,A}$, and asks whether field 1 is relatively more concentrated in $A$ than field 2. These cross-field comparisons are made for every pair of interaction fields that share a ligand or a receptor (11,718 pairs of fields). Along a transect the quantity is the deviation of the field from its own average over the transect, $\log M^{(d)}(\bs) - \log \bar{M}^{(d)}_{\text{transect}}$, at each position. Over the whole field of view it is either $\log S_1^{(d)}(\bs) - \log S_2^{(d)}(\bs)$ for two fields, or $\log S^{(d)}(\bs)$ for one field against its own average. The whole-field quantities are computed at each bin and, separately, after averaging $S^{(d)}$ within blocks of $5 \times 5$ bins in each draw (1,040 blocks); the block version gives up spatial resolution in exchange for power to detect structure larger than the fitted correlation range.

\subsubsection*{Inference}

For a single contrast, such as the log ratio of two regional averages, we report the posterior median and the 95\% equal-tailed interval, and we call a difference \textit{credible} when that interval excludes 1 on the ratio scale. For a contrast that varies along a transect or over the field of view, a location-by-location summary does not answer the question that is being asked of the map, which concerns many locations at once, and so the primary answer is joint. We report positive and negative excursion sets \cite{bolin2015excursion}: the largest set of locations at which the contrast exceeds zero (respectively falls below zero) simultaneously with posterior probability at least 0.95. We compute them from the posterior draws with the Monte Carlo routines of the \texttt{excursions} package \cite{bolin2018excursions}, which make no Gaussian assumption about the posterior. Locations are ordered by their estimated marginal probability of exceeding zero, and the set is grown in that order for as long as the fraction of draws in which every location already in the set exceeds zero remains at least 0.95. That fraction is itself a Monte Carlo estimate; with 1,000 draws its standard error near 0.95 is about 0.007, so the sets carry a margin of that order. Recomputing the whole-field sets of Fig~\ref{fig:wholefield} on the first and second 500 draws separately gave bin-level sets 6\% to 30\% larger than the full-draw sets, with the full-draw set almost entirely inside each, and block-level sets within 7\%; the sizes of bin-level sets for sparse fields should therefore be read as approximate to within roughly 10\% to 20\%, while their locations are stable. Along transects we also report a simultaneous 95\% credible band, formed from pointwise posterior quantiles at a common level chosen so that about 95\% of the draws lie inside the band at every position at once. Two secondary summaries show where a difference lies without making a joint claim: the pointwise 95\% interval at each position, and the set of locations whose own posterior probability of exceeding zero (or of falling below it) is at least 0.95. The latter is one-sided, at the same level as the excursion sets, so that the joint set is always a subset of the locations flagged pointwise.

\subsubsection*{The readings we compare against}

To show whether accounting for uncertainty changes a conclusion, each posterior statement is set beside three ways of answering the same question without the posterior.

\paragraph*{Reading the map.} Interaction fields are usually interpreted by eye from point-estimate maps. As an operational stand-in for that reading, we say a field \textit{looks different} between two regions when the ratio of its regional averages on the published (plug-in) map, the larger over the smaller, is at least a threshold $T$. We set $T = 1.25$ from the rulings of one author (AOZ) on five example maps with ratios between 1.16 and 2.94, judging differences in level only (S11 Fig). Two of the five rulings were uncertain, so every count is also reported at $T = 1.5$ and $T = 2$, both fixed in advance. The same threshold, applied to the absolute plug-in log ratio, defines \textit{looks different} at each transect position, bin and block.

\paragraph*{A rank test on the map, bins as independent observations.} For two regions, a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test at level 0.05 compares the plug-in values of the bins in one region with those in the other, as if each bin were an independent observation. For two fields within one region, a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test compares the two shape-normalized plug-in fields bin by bin. We apply the test to the smoothed map rather than to the observed counts for a practical reason. At this resolution the observed per-bin interaction score, $\min(y_L(\bs), y_R(\bs)) / \N(\bs)$, is zero at a median of 99.8\% of the bins of a region (90th percentile of the nonzero share 4.4\%), so the raw per-bin score is not something anyone reads; the smoothed map is. Testing a smoothed map location by location is nevertheless what is done whenever a kernel-smoothed or imputed field is compared between groups of spots, and this comparator stands for that practice. For completeness we also ran the rank-sum test on the raw per-bin scores and report the result.

\paragraph*{The posterior without its spatial correlation.} The rank test on the map ignores two things at once, the uncertainty at each bin and the correlation between bins. To separate them, we permuted each bin's 1,000 draws independently of every other bin's. This keeps each bin's marginal posterior exactly and removes only the posterior correlation between bins; the regional contrasts are then recomputed from the permuted draws. The difference between this comparator and the joint posterior is the effect of the correlation alone.

\subsubsection*{Examples and the systematic run}

The example fields shown in the figures were fixed before their posteriors were computed, so that they could not be chosen for the answer they give. Three ligand-receptor pairs were drawn at random, with a fixed seed, from the pairs that look different ($T = 1.25$) in a prespecified comparison and whose plug-in field is not nearly empty: CALR-ITGA2B for the heart against the liver, NID1-ITGB1 for the two spinal-cord boxes, and ADAM12-ITGB1 for the heart and liver parts of the heart-to-liver transect. For the cross-field example, one pair of fields sharing a ligand was drawn at random from the pairs in which neither field is nearly empty (CALM1-KCNQ3 and CALM1-PDE1A). The four pairs shown in S15 Fig (WNT4-FZD6, RSPO1-LGR6, DHH-PTCH1 and VEGFA-KDR) were added because they had been chosen for the original submission, long before any posterior comparison existed. For the single-gene example we used \textit{Cd44}, the representative feature of Fig~\ref{fig:raw_v_norm}, and, to isolate the effect of combining it with a partner, one of the 12 pairs containing \textit{CD44} whose field is not nearly empty, drawn at random (PKM-CD44). Here \textit{not nearly empty} means that the median of the plug-in field over the bins concerned is at least the median of the same quantity across all 1,477 pairs.

Every comparison was then run for all 1,477 pairs. The 790 refits were run on a shared computing cluster, and the posterior draws were stored as 16-bit integers on the log scale with a resolution of 0.001, which changes no reported quantity at the precision given. For each pair, the regional averages, the field-of-view average and the transect values of $M$ were computed within each draw, and every comparison and tabulation below was computed from those. The 91 instances (seven example pairs, 13 region comparisons each) that were computed both in a local pilot and in the cluster run, with different random seeds and on different machines, agree in 90 of 91 credible calls; the one disagreement has a lower interval endpoint near 1 in both runs (0.98 and 1.01).

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Raw counts, rather than normalized counts, are the appropriate input for spatial modeling}

Our experiments first indicated that commonly-applied count-normalization approaches can introduce topological artifacts when applied to these spatial data, a pattern also noted by others \cite{Bhuva2024}. Low individual-feature counts  can be transformed into local hotspots when the raw values are count-normalized within spots of low total RNA density. See Fig~\ref{fig:raw_v_norm} for a demonstration with a single feature, Cd44. We chose Cd44 for clarity rather than because it is special: the effect is visible there against an unambiguous anatomical landmark, the developing spine, which makes the raw and count-normalized panels easy to compare side by side. It is one clean example among many, not the only feature that shows the effect. The same comparison for a further 23 selected features is in S3 File, and the per-feature outputs for the whole curated set are in S2 File, so a reader can check how general the pattern is rather than taking one panel on trust. Because we aimed to model raw ligand-receptor \textit{binding density} within tissues, the results from this experiment made clear to us that it was most physically appropriate to convolve continuous ligand and receptor fields which estimate \textit{raw} ligand and receptor topology, rather than fields which estimate the commonly-used \textit{count-normalized} topology. Nevertheless, we decided to carry forward both approaches in parallel experimentally, so that we could identify and compare project-relevant differences downstream. As a secondary result, these early experiments also compared spatially modeling $\N(\bs)$, the nUMI, jointly with a ligand or receptor feature $F_i(\bs)$, against treating the observed nUMI as a fixed offset. Across the 23 features fitted both ways, the two give fitted fields with almost identical spatial pattern (Pearson correlation 0.986 to 0.997, median 0.995), modestly different levels (mean absolute difference 5.1\% to 13.5\% of the mean fitted count, median 6.6\%), and essentially identical posterior uncertainty (median ratio of posterior standard deviations 0.998, of 95\% interval widths 0.965). Since the downstream analyses depend on spatial pattern rather than absolute level, and since modeling the total jointly neither added nor removed appreciable uncertainty, the remainder of the results were run using the nUMI directly, as measured in the data, which is also much cheaper to fit.

\subsection*{Poisson likelihood model outperforms ZIP and ZAP models}

We compared three spatial modeling approaches: Poisson, ZIP, and ZAP (see \nameref{sec:meth-model-comp}). We found that the relative simplicity of the spatial Poisson model proved most suited, in these particular exercises, to the task of estimating feature counts. Table~\ref{tab:lik-comp} gives the four prediction scores by likelihood.

Of the four scores, we prioritized the two proper scoring rules, MDS and MLG, over the point-forecast measures MAE and RMSE, because what this framework offers downstream is a predictive distribution with quantified uncertainty rather than a point estimate, and only the proper scoring rules assess that distribution as a whole. In this particular comparison the choice of criterion does not change the conclusion, since the Poisson model gives the best average value on all four scores. This is not evident from the parenthetical percentages, which fall below 100\% for MDS. That is an artifact of expressing a negative score as a ratio: because MDS is negative, a ratio below 100\% corresponds to a score nearer zero and therefore worse, and the raw MDS values ($-2.15$ for Poisson against $-1.78$ for ZIP and $-1.77$ for ZAP) should be compared directly.

Nor is the average driven by a small number of extreme features. Comparing the three likelihoods feature by feature, across all 912 features and without the score filter applied to the table averages, the Poisson model gave the lowest score for 814 features on MAE, 880 on RMSE, 734 on MDS, and 829 on MLG, and was best on all four scores simultaneously for 645 of the 912. Predictive plots showed that the empirical distribution of the probability integral transform (PIT) values from the Poisson models was routinely closest to a uniform distribution, which is the distribution a calibrated predictive model would produce, when compared to the empirical distribution of the PIT values from the ZIP and ZAP models. Spatial residual plots revealed that the poorer average performance by the ZIP and ZAP models appears attributable to oversmoothing as evidenced by the predictions and residuals. See Fig~\ref{fig:model_comp} for comparison between models for a single feature, and S4 File for corresponding plots for all features.

We read this as evidence that the extra flexibility of a zero-inflated or zero-adjusted likelihood was not needed at the sparsity of this dataset at bin-50 resolution, not as a general result about count likelihoods for spatialomics. A platform or binning choice that produced substantially sparser data could well favor a zero-inflated likelihood, and the framework accommodates one.

\subsection*{The interaction fields are plug-in estimates, and differ from their posterior summaries}
\label{sec:plugin}

Before comparing interaction fields under uncertainty, we need to be clear about what the published maps of those fields are. Each published interaction field is a plug-in estimate: at every bin it is the smaller of the two partners' posterior medians, $\min(\tilde{L}(\bs), \tilde{R}(\bs))$, where $\tilde{L}(\bs)$ and $\tilde{R}(\bs)$ denote the posterior medians of the ligand's and the receptor's expected count at bin $\bs$. The quantity of interest is the interaction itself, $M(\bs) = \min(L(\bs), R(\bs))$, whose posterior is not summarized by the minimum of two medians. Because each feature is fitted separately, the ligand and receptor posteriors are independent, so at each bin $P(M(\bs) > t) = P(L(\bs) > t)\,P(R(\bs) > t)$, and the posterior of $M(\bs)$ follows from the two marginal posteriors alone. Posterior draws were not stored, so we approximated each marginal posterior by a lognormal distribution matched to its stored median and 95\% interval, which corresponds to a Gaussian posterior on the scale of the linear predictor. The approximation is adequate for most bins: on the log scale the stored intervals are close to symmetric (median ratio of upper to lower half-width 0.995, with 90\% of bins between 0.85 and 1.12, in a random sample of 60 features), and the implied posterior means agree with the stored ones to within 10\% for 90\% of feature-bin combinations. We later checked it against the joint posterior draws described in the \nameref{sec:meth-draws} section for the seven example pairs: the approximate and draw-based posterior medians of $M(\bs)$ differ by a median of 0.02 to 0.06 on the log scale per pair (90th percentile 0.05 to 0.15; Pearson correlation over bins at least 0.997), which is comparable to the Monte Carlo error of a per-bin median from 1,000 draws. The percentages below should therefore be read as approximate to a few percentage points.

    \centering

    \label{fig:plugin}
\end{figure}

Relative to the posterior median of $M(\bs)$, the plug-in field is never lower. This holds exactly: with $m^* = \min(\tilde{L}, \tilde{R})$, $P(M > m^*) = P(L > m^*)\,P(R > m^*) \le 1/2$. Across all 1,477 pairs and 26,000 bins the overstatement has a median of 1.3\%, but it is heavy-tailed: it exceeds 10\% in 35\% of pair-bin combinations and 25\% in 22\% (Fig~\ref{fig:plugin}). It is concentrated where neither partner is clearly the limiting one, that is, where the two marginal posteriors overlap; the per-pair Spearman correlation between the overstatement and the standardized gap between the partners' log-scale posterior medians has a median of $-0.98$. Relative to the posterior mean of $M(\bs)$ the direction reverses: the marginal posteriors are wide and right-skewed (median log-scale standard deviation 1.03 in the same sample), so their means lie well above their medians, and the plug-in field is below the posterior mean of $M(\bs)$ in a median of 91\% of bins per pair.

For the analyses reported here, the more relevant question is whether these differences change spatial pattern, since each field is scaled before clustering. They change it little. Per pair, the Pearson correlation over bins between the plug-in field and the posterior median of $M(\bs)$ has a median of 0.988 (5th percentile 0.963), and between the plug-in field and the posterior mean of $M(\bs)$ a median of 0.967 (5th percentile 0.905). We therefore expect the domains to change little if the plug-in fields were replaced by either posterior summary, but we have not re-run the clustering to confirm this. What the plug-in map cannot tell us is how far the field at one place could plausibly be from the field at another, which is the question the following sections ask. They use the joint posterior draws described in the \nameref{sec:meth-draws} section, which remove the need for the lognormal approximation; the summaries in this subsection, which concern the plug-in operator itself, were not recomputed from the draws.

\subsection*{Region comparisons}
\label{sec:res-regions}

We begin with the simplest question that is asked of an interaction-field map: is the field higher in one region than in another? Fig~\ref{fig:regions} shows the regions and transects that were fixed in advance (panel A) and, for the three ligand-receptor pairs drawn at random and for the single gene \textit{Cd44}, sets the plug-in reading of each of the 13 region comparisons against its posterior (panel B). Two features are visible at once. The interaction fields are far less certain than the single-gene field: across the 91 region comparisons of the seven example pairs, the 95\% interval for the ratio of regional averages spans a median factor of 1.75 (upper over lower endpoint), against a factor of 1.03 to 1.13 for every \textit{Cd44} comparison. And several differences that look clear on the map are not supported by the posterior. For ADAM12-ITGB1, for example, the field is 1.51 times higher in the first heart box than in the second on the plug-in map, and a rank-sum test treating the 225 bins of each box as independent observations gives $p = 2.8 \times 10^{-4}$, yet the posterior 95\% interval for the ratio runs from 0.72 to 2.64. Every \textit{Cd44} comparison, in contrast, is credible, including three whose plug-in ratio is below the 1.25 threshold and which would therefore be read from the map as alike.

The pattern seen in the examples holds across all 1,477 pairs (Table~\ref{tab:systematic}). Of the 19,201 region comparisons (1,477 pairs, 13 comparisons each), 11,527 look different at $T = 1.25$, and for 3,603 of those (31\%) the posterior interval for the ratio includes 1. The share falls as the threshold rises, to 20\% at $T = 1.5$ and 10\% at $T = 2$, but it does not vanish. It depends strongly on the size and kind of the regions compared. Among comparisons that look different, the share that is not credible is 91\% for the two spinal-cord boxes and 83\% for the two heart boxes, each a comparison of two small regions within one tissue, where a visible difference is most likely to be noise; for pairs of whole anatomical regions it is 10\% to 37\%. The reverse also occurs, less often: 991 comparisons look alike on the map but are credibly different, with posterior median ratios that are mostly small (median 1.26; 10th to 90th percentile 1.14 to 1.58).

The readings that do not use the posterior disagree with it in a consistent direction (Table~\ref{tab:readings}). A rank-sum test on the plug-in map, treating bins as independent observations, finds a difference ($p < 0.05$) in 17,320 of the 19,201 comparisons (90\%); the joint posterior finds one in 8,915 (46\%). The posterior with its between-bin correlation removed, which keeps every bin's own uncertainty, finds 14,006 (73\%). Removing the correlation alone therefore moves the posterior 27 of the 44 percentage points from the joint posterior toward the rank test, and we read this as most of the gap being attributable to the correlation rather than to the per-bin uncertainty, with the caveat that a rank test and a posterior interval are different statistics and the split is indicative rather than exact. The same comparison for the seven example pairs shows the mechanism directly: with the correlation removed, their intervals narrowed by a median factor of 2.7 on the log scale (range 1.9 to 6.0). The rank-sum test applied instead to the observed per-bin scores, which are zero at almost every bin, behaves very differently: it is undefined in 3,086 comparisons where both regions have no nonzero bin, finds a difference in 7,300 (38\%), and finds one in only 483 of the 3,603 comparisons that look different but are not credible. The 90\% figure is thus a property of testing smoothed values as if they were independent, not of rank tests on these data in general; on the raw scores the test is weak rather than anticonservative.

Comparisons between two different interaction fields behave the same way as comparisons within one. Among the 11,718 pairs of fields that share a ligand or a receptor, compared within each of ten regions after dividing each field by its own average, 69,723 of 117,180 comparisons look different at $T = 1.25$ and 27,167 of those (39\%) are not credible. None of these counts is a rate over a population of tissues or of pairs: they come from one tissue section, and the comparisons share regions and genes. The 17 pairs involving a gene whose refit differed from the published fit change no count by more than about 1\% (S13 Table).

\subsection*{Transects}

A transect asks a different question of the map: not whether two named regions differ, but where along a line a field departs from its own average, which is a statement about many positions at once. Fig~\ref{fig:transects} shows the answer for the heart-to-liver transect, for the pair drawn at random for this transect and for \textit{Cd44}. On the plug-in map, ADAM12-ITGB1 varies by a factor of 15 along the transect (largest over smallest value), and a reader of the map would see a field that rises in the heart and again near the liver. \subsection*{Whole fields}

The third form of the question concerns a whole map: where is a field credibly above or below a reference, with a single probability statement covering every location named? The joint excursion sets answer this (Fig~\ref{fig:wholefield}). For the two fields drawn as the cross-field example, CALM1-KCNQ3 and CALM1-PDE1A, each divided by its own average, 2,612 of the 26,000 bins are credibly different when bins are examined one at a time, but only about 200 (198, with the Monte Carlo margin noted in the \nameref{sec:meth-uq} section) belong to the joint sets. The spinal cord, where the plug-in map shows a clear band of one field over the other, appears in the bin-level joint sets only as scattered bins. Averaging within $5\times5$-bin blocks before the comparison recovers it: 63 of the 1,040 blocks are in the joint sets, including a run of blocks along the spinal cord, and this agrees with the region comparison for the same two fields, in which the spinal cord is credibly different (posterior median ratio 4.32, 95\% interval 3.74 to 4.95). The two resolutions answer slightly different questions. A bin-level joint set requires every bin in it to clear the threshold at once, which is a demanding statement for structure much larger than the correlation range of the field; the block-level set asks the same of block averages, giving up resolution for power.

For \textit{Cd44} against its own average, 8,825 bins are in the joint sets, and the anatomy is plain in them. For PKM-CD44, the interaction field formed by taking the minimum of the \textit{Cd44} field and a partner gene field, the number falls to 2,084. With one randomly drawn partner this illustrates, rather than measures, how much certainty a single-gene field can lose once it is combined with a second gene.

The difference from the geometric mean is within the run-to-run variation of the clustering, so those two should not be read as ranked. To ask whether tissue spatial domains reflect the structure of the high-dimensional collection of interaction fields rather than of a few individual mechanisms, we performed a targeted parameter sweep experiment in which we iteratively increased the number of signaling features, ranking them by variance, while holding all else equal. We iteratively added more features to our analysis and quantified the output number of clusters and their spatial entropy to observe changes in spatial domain architecture in both histologic space and a uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embedding. Our results are summarized in Fig~\ref{fig:spatial_domains}, and full results may be found in S7 File.

In this tissue section, the recovered spatial domain architecture depended on many inferred interaction fields at once rather than on a small number of them, though not in the simple form the variance-ordered sweep suggests. In that sweep (Fig~\ref{fig:spatial_domains}) the number of domains fell as fields were added, from 33 at 10 fields to 24 at 150 and between 16 and 21 from 350 onward. Mean ELSA at radius 50 was not monotone: it rose from 0.143 at 10 fields to a peak of 0.220 at 100, then fell to 0.124 at full depth. Full depth was not the most coherent depth swept; five deeper depths (1200, 1250, 1350, 1400 and 1450 fields) scored between 0.109 and 0.112, although at that end of the curve differences between depths are not much larger than the run-to-run variation of the clustering (see the \nameref{sec:controls} section).

The random-subset sweep separates the number of fields from their identity. With fields admitted at random, mean ELSA at radius 50 fell steadily with depth: 0.372 at 10 fields (standard deviation across the 19 subsets 0.044), 0.240 at 50 (0.034), 0.168 at 150 (0.029), 0.134 at 350 (0.032), 0.135 at 650 (0.024) and 0.120 at 1000 (0.012). Most of the improvement arrives by a few hundred fields, with slower change thereafter. The rise and fall seen in the variance-ordered sweep does not appear when fields are admitted at random, which suggests that it reflects the order of admission rather than the number of fields. The two sweeps were run separately, and the random-subset sweep drew from the 1449 distinct fields, so we compare the shapes of the two curves rather than their values at matching depths. All of these clusterings were run without principal-component analysis (PCA); we did not run a matched comparison with PCA, and so make no claim about whether omitting it helps. At full depth the domain map resolves local detail, such as inter-organ fascial planes, that is not apparent at the shallowest depths.

We examined the inferred hyperfield structure in a small sub-region of the embryo, outlined by a dashed white box in Fig~\ref{fig:spatial_domains}. Selected feature-level topologies (individual inferred ligand-receptor interaction fields) have been rendered as three-dimensional surfaces at the bottom of Fig~\ref{fig:spatial_domains}. HTML files for every interaction field within this region, allowing user-interactive zoom, value estimation and rotation in space, are available in S8 File.

\subsubsection*{Controls}
\label{sec:controls}

Three controls bear on what these domains show; they are described in full in S16 Appendix, and the first two are summarized in S10 Fig. First, a spatial null: we permuted the assignment of the observed counts to locations, refitted all 912 features from scratch on the permuted data, and passed them through the same convolution, clustering and scoring. The observed data gave 20 domains with mean ELSA at radius 50 of 0.075; the 49 permutations gave 4 to 12 domains with ELSA from 0.412 to 0.583, a permutation $p$ of 0.020, the smallest attainable with 49 permutations. Both arms of this control were clustered with a faster implementation (a nearest-neighbor graph from RcppHNSW and Louvain clustering in igraph) whose ELSA values are not on the same scale as those in Fig~\ref{fig:spatial_domains}. Second, agreement with independent anatomy: the domains agree with the anatomical annotation published for the same section \cite{Chen2022} well above chance (adjusted Rand index 0.401, normalized mutual information 0.604; $p = 0.002$ from 499 permutations), while subdividing several organs, most visibly the heart. Third, the ligand-receptor pairing: assigning receptors to ligands at random did not make the domains less coherent (in Seurat, 17 of 19 shuffled pairings gave more coherent domains than the true pairing), so the domains reflect the collection of fitted ligand and receptor fields rather than the specific pairings, although true partners are more alike in spatial pattern than random ones (mean Pearson correlation 0.111 against 0.068, paired $t$-test $p = 4\times10^{-21}$). Finally, independent re-runs of the Seurat clustering differed from the published values by up to about 0.01 in mean ELSA, so differences of that size between single runs should not be read as meaningful.

\section*{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Interaction fields estimated from spatial transcriptomics are usually read from point-estimate maps, and the central finding of this paper is that, on the section studied here, those readings and the posterior often disagree. Of the region comparisons that look different on the maps at a ratio of 1.25, about three in ten are not supported by the posterior, and the share is far higher for small regions within one tissue, where it reaches about nine in ten, than for whole organs (Table~\ref{tab:systematic}). Along transects and over whole fields, statements that hold jointly mark far fewer locations than statements made location by location (Figs~\ref{fig:transects} and \ref{fig:wholefield}). We read these as demonstrations of what a posterior over the fields makes possible on one section, not as estimates of how often published readings of other data are wrong. The comparisons share regions and genes; the threshold for \textit{looks different} is a stand-in for reading a map by eye that rests on one author's rulings on five examples; and part of the gap between map and posterior is the upward bias of the plug-in operator (Fig~\ref{fig:plugin}) rather than uncertainty. A comparison that is not credible has not been shown to be absent, either: it is one for which these data, under this model, do not establish a direction.

Much of the disagreement comes from treating neighboring locations as if they carried independent information. The fitted correlation ranges of the genes behind our example pairs span roughly 2 to 9 bins, so a box of $15\times15$ bins holds far fewer independent pieces of information about a field's level than its 225 bins suggest. When we removed only the correlation between bins from the posterior, keeping each bin's own uncertainty, the intervals for regional ratios became about 2.7 times narrower (median, on the log scale) and the share of comparisons called different rose from 46\% to 73\%, most of the way to the 90\% found by a rank-sum test that treats the bins of the smoothed map as independent observations (Table~\ref{tab:readings}). The construct tested matters here. The 90\% figure describes what happens when a smoothed field, from any source, is tested location by location; the same rank test on the raw per-bin scores, which are zero at nearly every bin at this resolution, finds a difference in 38\% of comparisons and is weak rather than anticonservative. Our concern is therefore with the smoothed-then-tested practice, and our results are consistent with simulation studies of related settings. For differential expression between cell types in imaging-based spatial data, Vasconcelos et al.\ \cite{vasconcelos2026smide} report type I error rates of 0.86 to 0.95 at a nominal 0.05 for methods that ignore spatial correlation, against near-nominal rates for a Gaussian-process model, and Seal and Neelon \cite{seal2026spacebf} report inflated type I error for correlation-based co-expression statistics, including bivariate Moran's $I$ and Lee's $L$, when both genes are spatially autocorrelated. Several widely used methods compute significance under an explicit independence assumption, for example across pixels in C-SIDE \cite{cable2022cside} or across cell locations in the permutation null of CytoSignal \cite{Liu2026}. We have not run those methods, and the assumption may matter less for their targets than for ours, but our results suggest that how well such nulls are calibrated under spatial correlation is worth checking.

The joint statements have limits of their own. An excursion set requires every location in it to clear the threshold simultaneously, and the sets are built by adding locations in order of their marginal probability, an ordering that, as Bolin and Lindgren note, does not use the spatial dependence directly \cite{bolin2015excursion}. Bin-level sets are therefore conservative for structures much larger than the correlation range: the spinal-cord band in Fig~\ref{fig:wholefield} is credibly different as a region but appears only as scattered bins, and averaging within blocks recovers it at lower resolution. A statement about a connected region as a whole, in the spirit of cluster-extent inference in functional neuroimaging, would use spatial coherence more directly. A first variant we tried, which required each bin to lie in a sufficiently large connected region in each posterior draw, added nothing over the bin-level sets, and a faithful version is future work. All of these statements also inherit the approximations and assumptions of the fitted model: the integrated nested Laplace approximation to the joint posterior; for 12 sparsely detected genes, refits that did not reproduce the published fits within Monte Carlo error; the penalized-complexity priors on the range and marginal standard deviation; and a single stationary Mat\'ern field per gene. We checked the priors on the 91 regional comparisons of the seven example pairs by refitting their 13 genes with the range prior halved, with it doubled, and with the prior on the marginal standard deviation relaxed by a factor of ten ($P(\sigma_i > 1) = 0.05$): the first two changed no credible call and left the median interval width unchanged to within 0.5\%, and the third changed two calls of 91 (both from credible to not credible) with a median width ratio of 0.99 (range 0.67 to 1.15), which is of the same order as the one call that changes between two halves of the posterior draws. The regional conclusions are therefore not sensitive to these priors on this section, as far as that check reaches. On the last point, Velidi et al.\ \cite{velidi2026nonstationarity} report in a recent preprint that, across 12 Visium datasets, between 3\% and 50\% of genes are better described by a non-stationary covariance. We have not tested stationarity on these data, and where it fails, intervals and excursion sets could be too wide in some parts of the tissue and too narrow in others.

A central question in tissue biology is how and why tissues develop, retain, and lose their form. This broad question is fundamental to developmental biology, tissue engineering, regenerative medicine, and multicellular biology across species. For the better part of a century, we have understood that extracellular signaling plays a critical role in the patterning and organization of cells within tissues. Local intercellular cues preserve adult stem cell niches, govern cellular differentiation, and regulate tissue response to injury. However, despite this knowledge, our ability to \textit{understand} cellular communication in tissues, i.e., to deliberately influence it to induce healing or desired change, is woefully limited.

Recent research has revealed that the grammar of cellular communication is intrinsically high-dimensional, with cells communicating on hundreds-to-thousands of wavelengths at once and processing/interpreting these cues via whole-cell parallel processing \cite{Granados2024}. This means that in order to properly model and visualize cell-to-cell communication, we must be comfortable observing and interpreting high-dimensional signaling 'hyper-fields' as single, holistic objects of study in histologic space. The work in this paper has been carried out with this principle in mind.

We see the outputs from these models as the start of a growing foundation for what we envision as 'morphogenic field analysis': the interdisciplinary study of how morphogenic field interactions, gradients, dynamics, and perturbations shape tissue organization and control cellular differentiation, ideally in three-dimensions over time. Although the data analysis shown here is exclusively two-dimensional, and does not include a time dimension, there are many datasets emerging which do allow spatiotemporal analysis and there are incoming tools allowing affordable 3D profiling \cite{Reynolds2025}. The geostatistical framework we use extends in principle to three spatial dimensions and to a time dimension, since the same Gaussian-process machinery is routinely applied in those settings in other fields, though we have not done so here. Applied to timeseries datasets, longitudinal modeling could be used to ask whether tissue field dynamics precede, align with, or follow cellular growth and change during tissue remodeling.

We have demonstrated this approach on a single field of view, from one section of one embryo, on one platform, at one binning resolution, and we do not want to claim more reach than that supports. It seems more useful to state what we think would need to hold for the approach to transfer. The observations must be counts accompanied by an interpretable exposure or offset, since the model treats the local total as known sampling effort. The spatial support must be dense enough that a Gaussian process is identifiable at the length scales of interest, which is a joint property of a platform's resolution, its capture efficiency, and the binning applied. Platforms with substantially sparser capture than the data used here may favor a zero-inflated likelihood, which our comparison found unnecessary at this sparsity but which the framework accommodates. At single-cell-resolution and imaging-based platforms, where segmentation rather than binning defines the observation unit, the binning step we rely on would need to be replaced by an explicit treatment of cell geometry; spot-based and imaging-based data should not be treated identically, because their measurement methods and organization differ. Tissue-specific customization is likewise possible but is not automatic: we are separately experimenting with an adaptation for lung tissue that excludes large lumens and alveolar airspaces from the tissue fields.

Two features of the framework are worth separating from the demonstration itself, because they are not specific to the platform used here. The model requires only counts at known locations together with a measure of local sampling effort, and the stochastic partial differential equation representation of the Gaussian process is defined on a triangulated mesh rather than on a lattice, so an irregular, hexagonal, or otherwise non-gridded arrangement of observation locations requires no special handling. We have separately fitted spatial fields of this kind to 10x Visium mouse brain sections, whose hexagonal spot array and much coarser spatial resolution differ substantially from the binned nanoball array used here, obtaining fields for 2,035 ligand-receptor mechanisms across two sagittal sections, with the posterior predictive occurrence probabilities tracking the observed occurrence closely across all 3,865 fits (correlation 0.99, median absolute difference 0.015). We report this as an indication that the field-estimation step is not tied to one platform's geometry, and deliberately not as a validation of the pipeline as a whole: that work used a two-part semicontinuous observation model on normalized data rather than the raw-count model developed here, and it did not carry the fitted fields through the convolution and clustering stages. Comprehensive evaluation across tissues and platforms remains future work.

Applying the framework to diseased tissue is a natural next step, and one motivation for developing it, but it is future work rather than something this manuscript demonstrates. This work is a methodological proof of concept on healthy developing tissue. Doing the diseased case properly would require matched healthy and diseased tissue, attention to the confounding between disease state and tissue composition, and enough replication to separate a disease effect from between-sample variation. Carried out on a single additional section, it would yield an illustrative figure that we could not defend statistically, which is the opposite of what we are arguing for here.

\subsection*{Relationship to existing methods}

Several established methods place a Gaussian process over space in transcriptomic data, and it is worth stating precisely which parts of the present approach are new and which are combinations or reinterpretations of existing ideas.

SpatialDE \cite{Svensson2018} introduced Gaussian-process modeling for spatial transcriptomics, decomposing each gene's expression variance into a spatial component parameterized by a covariance kernel and a non-spatial noise component, then testing the spatial component by a likelihood ratio test. We share its use of a Gaussian process prior over space, but not its inferential target: SpatialDE asks which genes are spatially variable, whereas we estimate the field itself and then use it downstream.

BOOST-GP \cite{Li2021} is the closest methodological relative. It places a gene-specific zero-mean Gaussian process on the spatial random effect inside a zero-inflated negative binomial count model, with Bayesian variable selection over whether that spatial component is present, and its stated goal is likewise the identification of spatially variable genes. The combination of a Gaussian process with a Bayesian count likelihood is therefore not something we claim as novel. What differs is what is done with the posterior: we retain the fitted fields as continuous objects with quantified uncertainty, convolve them pairwise, and analyze the resulting high-dimensional collection jointly.

Several Bayesian models report posterior uncertainty for spatial expression but use it for other purposes than comparing locations. BayesTME \cite{zhang2023bayestme} uses posterior samples to select marker genes and spatial transcriptional programs with control of the Bayesian false discovery rate, BOOST-GP reports a posterior probability that each gene is spatially variable, and MEFISTO \cite{velten2022mefisto} provides uncertainty for interpolating latent factors. In what we read, none of them uses the posterior to compare regions, transects or whole fields.

Splotch \cite{aijo2019splotch} is the closest precedent for comparing regions. It is a Bayesian zero-inflated Poisson model of each gene with anatomical region effects and a conditional autoregressive spatial term, fitted by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, and it compares two regions through the posterior of the difference of their region coefficients, summarized by a Savage-Dickey Bayes factor, one pair of regions at a time. In our approach the regions are not part of the model, so any region, transect or derived quantity, including the minimum of two genes' fields, can be compared after a single fit per gene, and transects and whole fields are summarized by joint statements rather than one comparison at a time.

For joint statements, the excursion sets we use \cite{bolin2015excursion,bolin2018excursions} were applied with the same integrated nested Laplace approximation and SPDE fields to functional brain imaging on the cortical surface by Mejia et al.\ \cite{mejia2020bayesian}, which is the closest precedent for our inferential machinery. SHADE \cite{eliason2026shade} builds simultaneous credible bands from joint posterior draws for interaction curves between cell types in multiplexed imaging, the closest precedent for our transect bands. SpaceBF \cite{seal2026spacebf} places pointwise credible intervals on a spatially varying co-expression slope between a ligand and a receptor, the closest precedent among ligand-receptor methods that report posterior uncertainty. None of these combines a posterior over continuous expression fields, ligand-receptor interaction fields derived from it, and joint comparisons over regions, transects and whole fields.

Spatial Variance Component Analysis (SVCA) \cite{Arnol2019} fits an additive Gaussian-process random-effect model that decomposes the variance of each gene or protein into intrinsic, environmental, cell-cell interaction, and noise components, and tests whether the interaction component is nonzero. Its aim is variance partitioning rather than field estimation, and it was developed for imaging-based data.

SpatialDM \cite{Li2023} is the closest in biological target. It tests ligand-receptor pairs for spatial co-expression using a bivariate extension of Moran's $I$, both globally, selecting which pairs are spatially co-expressed across the tissue, and locally, identifying the spots that drive the association, with significance from either a permutation null or an analytically derived one. The analytical null is derived under the assumption that each gene is spatially randomly distributed, and in the applications we read local spots were called at an unadjusted per-spot threshold. It targets the significance of individual interactions, whereas we target a continuous inferred field per interaction, and then cluster across all of them at once.

CytoSignal \cite{Liu2026} appeared after our original submission and analyzes the same Stereo-seq mouse embryo atlas. It computes a per-cell ligand-receptor score as the product of spatially aggregated ligand and receptor expression, using a Gaussian kernel for diffusion-dependent interactions and Delaunay neighbors for contact-dependent ones, assesses significance against a spatial-permutation null, and validates predicted interactions against proximity ligation assays performed on adjacent tissue sections. It is the one method in this list that, like ours, produces a spatially resolved signaling quantity rather than a per-feature test statistic. In light of that work we do not claim novelty for spatially resolved ligand-receptor mapping. Our operator comparison (S15 Fig) is relevant here, in that the common minimum yielded more spatially coherent domains on this dataset than the product operator that CytoSignal uses, but that is a comparison of two convolution operators inside our own pipeline, not a comparison of the two methods.

Stating our contribution precisely, and more narrowly than our original submission did, it consists of three parts. The first is a generative Bayesian geostatistical treatment of raw spatialomics counts, which yields a posterior for every gene field and, through joint posterior draws, for every ligand-receptor interaction field. The second is the use of those posteriors, with joint inference, to ask whether differences visible on interaction-field maps are supported by the data, for regional averages, along transects and over whole fields, with the regions and examples fixed before any comparison was computed. The third is the finding that clustering the full collection of interaction fields produces tissue domains that recover major anatomical structures while resolving substructure within them. None of the ingredients is new on its own: Gaussian-process models of counts, excursion sets and Bayesian comparisons of regions all exist, as described above. What we offer is their combination, applied to ligand-receptor interaction fields. As the pair-shuffling control shows, the domains do not depend on the specific ligand-receptor pairings, which bounds what the third part says about signaling.

The pair-shuffling control (see the \nameref{sec:controls} section) limits what the domains can be said to show. Shuffling which receptor is paired with which ligand did not make the domains less coherent, even though true partners are measurably more alike in spatial pattern than random partners, and true pairings yield more distinct fields than shuffled ones. The domains reported here are therefore best read as a property of the collection of fitted ligand and receptor fields as combined by the common minimum on raw counts, and not as evidence that the specific ligand-receptor pairings shape them. We suspect the operator is largely responsible, though we have not tested this directly. On raw counts one partner of a pair is often the smaller of the two almost everywhere (for 42.6\% of pairs, at more than 95\% of locations), so the common minimum frequently returns that partner's field with only limited regions carved out of it, and little of what distinguishes a true partner from a random one need reach the clustering. Whether a different operator, or the same operator applied to fields placed on a common scale, would carry the pairing through is a question for future work.

One limit of the domain analyses remains. The clustering operates on the plug-in interaction fields, not on posterior draws, so the domains do not carry propagated uncertainty. The posterior comparisons in this paper propagate uncertainty for regional averages, transects and whole fields; carrying it through the clustering itself, so that the domains come with uncertainty, is a computation we have not done.

We have not run a head-to-head benchmark against any of these methods, and we would rather be direct about that than leave it implicit. They answer different questions: SpatialDE and BOOST-GP test genes for spatial variability, SpatialDM tests ligand-receptor pairs for spatial association, and SVCA partitions variance into components. There is no shared target quantity on which an accuracy comparison would be meaningful without first defining a benchmark task and a ground truth, which we regard as a separate piece of work. A reader who wants a significance test for individual features or individual interactions should use one of the methods above; the framework presented here is aimed at estimating the fields and analyzing them jointly.

The statistical models presented here have some limitations. First, they must be tuned to each input type of data: Xenium, Visium, Stereo-seq (shown here), CurioTrekker, CosMx, COMET, and others. We have fitted the model only to Stereo-seq data in this work, so our expectation that adapting it to other platforms is straightforward remains an expectation rather than a demonstrated result. Second, we currently are ignoring three major considerations: 1. each signaling mechanism is treated the same (there is no incorporation of molecule-specific diffusion coefficients based on molecular weight), 2. there is no accommodation for variable extracellular matrix properties (competitive binding, protein-density-diffusion limitations), and 3. there is no accommodation for ligand-receptor sequestration due to binding (complex non-linear modeling required). These deficits shall be addressed in future studies, and present many interesting opportunities for further experimentation.

``All models are wrong, but some are useful". The modeling outputs shown here are a \textit{proxy} for high-dimensional information flow \textit{potential} within tissues. Stated plainly, the interaction fields estimate the potential for signaling under the co-expression assumption standard in cell-cell communication analysis, that a ligand and its receptor expressed in the same place have the opportunity to interact. We propose them as a proxy for signaling; we do not observe signaling. It is worth setting out the inferential chain explicitly, so a reader can see how far these outputs sit from the measurement. Transcript counts are a proxy for local transcript abundance, subject to the sampling variability this framework is designed to model. Transcript abundance is in turn a proxy for protein abundance, a step which is known to be imperfect and which we do not attempt to correct. Co-localization of a ligand proxy and a receptor proxy within the same bin of 50 by 50 capture spots, rather than at a shared cell surface, is a proxy for the \textit{opportunity} for those molecules to interact, and the convolution operators we compare are alternative ways of quantifying that opportunity under stated stoichiometric assumptions. None of the intermediate quantities, and no binding event, is directly observed in this work. Further data must be acquired at the protein level and the intracellular level before any of these model outputs are treated as evidence of actual information transmission or transduction. The experimental cost of doing this, however, can be high, particularly if the patterns of interest are high-dimensional and cannot be reduced to targeted cell-scale, pathway-scale, or molecule-scale experiments. Therefore, in our opinion, the value of the modeling approach described in this paper lies not in its ability to predict single-mechanism interactions. Rather, it lies in its potential to support mappings, with quantified uncertainty, between estimates of the holistic high-dimensional extracellular signaling milieu and cell-state-specific intracellular state changes and transitions. A cell within a tissue experiences hundreds to thousands of cues at once, and processes these cues in parallel, as a single, holistic, high-dimensional signal. Our modeling approach allows us to locally estimate a transcript-derived proxy for this high-dimensional signal while providing measures of statistical uncertainty. When paired with \textit{intra}cellular data, such as that regarding transcription factor activation, cytoskeletal remodeling, metabolic reprogramming and mechanoresponse, we are newly provided with an opportunity to quantitatively study the precise ways that different cells, in different states, process and respond to high-dimensional dynamics in their local extracellular milieu: that is, how the holistic cellular microenvironment governs cellular state, and therefore how extracellular milieus might be targeted in the future to induce regenerative healing.

Our code is open access on GitHub, and we have included all of our work from this project so that others can rerun our analyses, see how we have organized our inputs and outputs, and experiment on their own. We note that rerunning the clustering stage may not reproduce the published domain map exactly (see the \nameref{sec:controls} section); the fitted fields and the stored domain labels are provided so that downstream work does not depend on reproducing it. The images presented in the figures of this paper are a small fraction of our model outputs, and we encourage interested readers to download the supporting information included with this paper as a way to explore the scale and structure of the inferred interaction fields. We look forward to working with the community to explore morphogenic field analysis in additional tissue datasets and to brainstorm ways to influence spatial extracellular signaling for therapeutic means.

(D) The corresponding `entrogram', a map of the entropy-based local indicator of spatial association (ELSA) \cite{naimi2019elsa} at radius 50 computed on the domain labels and scaled from 0 to 1; high values mark locations where domain labels are locally mixed, so coherent domains appear as dark interiors bounded by thin bright borders. (E) Mean ELSA against the ELSA neighborhood radius for a subset of the representations tested. (F) Mean ELSA at radius 100 against the number of clusters recovered, one point per representation tested, with the common minimum in red. Lower ELSA and more clusters are both preferred, so the favorable region is the lower right. Of the operators tested, the common minimum gave the largest number of clusters, with ELSA close to the lowest (that of the geometric mean) and well below that of the product.
